# Capital Structure and WACC

Module: Corporate Issuers and Equity Valuation

## Lesson summary

Capital structure allocates operating risk and cash flows among debt, equity,
and other claims. Debt can provide a tax benefit and impose discipline, but it
also creates fixed payments, refinancing exposure, covenants, collateral
claims, and distress costs. Equity absorbs residual volatility and requires a
higher expected return when leverage increases.

This lesson develops a market-value WACC and target-leverage analysis. The
examples are simulations in USD millions. Cash flows occur at year-end and
discount rates are effective annual USD rates on a nominal, inflation-inclusive
basis.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, readers should be able to:

- explain the Modigliani–Miller benchmark and the assumptions behind capital
  structure irrelevance;
- connect taxes, distress costs, agency conflicts, information, and financial
  flexibility to target leverage;
- estimate cost of equity with beta and an equity risk premium;
- estimate the marginal pretax and after-tax cost of debt;
- unlever and relever beta for a target debt/equity ratio;
- calculate WACC using market-value weights;
- identify currency, tax, maturity, and project-risk mismatches in a discount
  rate.

## Prerequisites and notation

Complete [Corporate Decisions and Capital Budgeting](5.1.corporate_decisions_and_capital_budgeting.md).

- $D$: market value of interest-bearing debt and aligned debt-like claims;
- $E$: market value of common equity;
- $k_d$: marginal pretax cost of debt;
- $k_e$: cost of common equity;
- $\tau_{\mathrm{shield}}$: marginal tax rate expected to produce a usable
  interest deduction;
- $r_f$: risk-free rate in the cash flow's currency and horizon;
- $\mathrm{ERP}$: equity risk premium;
- $\beta_L$, $\beta_U$: levered and unlevered equity beta.

Rates are decimals in calculations. Book values are not substitutes for market
values merely because they are easier to obtain.

If a source quotes a nominal annual percentage rate $j^{(m)}$ compounded $m$
times per year, convert it before using annual discount periods:

```{math}
r_{\mathrm{EA}}
=\left(1+\frac{j^{(m)}}{m}\right)^m-1.
```

Here $r_{\mathrm{EA}}$ is the effective annual rate. This compounding conversion
is separate from the nominal-versus-real inflation convention.

## The capital structure benchmark

Under the Modigliani–Miller benchmark without taxes, distress, transaction
costs, information differences, or agency conflicts—and with investors and
companies able to borrow on comparable terms—the value of the operating assets
does not depend on the mix of debt and equity. Leverage redistributes risk
rather than creating operating cash flow
{cite}`modiglianiMiller1958CostCapital`.

As debt/equity increases, equity becomes riskier because debt holders have a
prior fixed claim. A simplified relation is:

```{math}
k_e
=k_0+(k_0-k_d)\frac{D}{E},
```

where $k_0$ is the required return on an unlevered operating asset under the
benchmark assumptions.

With deductible interest, debt can create a tax shield. In practice, the
benefit is limited by taxable income, tax rules, loss carryforwards, debt caps,
jurisdiction, refinancing, and the probability that the deduction is usable.
More leverage also increases expected distress costs and can alter operating
behavior before formal default
{cite}`modiglianiMiller1963CorporateTaxes,krausLitzenberger1973Leverage,jensen1986AgencyFreeCashFlow`.

## Why target leverage is a range

Consider:

- **Business risk:** cyclicality, operating leverage, customer concentration,
  commodity exposure, regulation, and disruption.
- **Asset support:** collateral value, asset duration, redeployability, and
  recovery uncertainty.
- **Cash-flow resilience:** recurring revenue, working-capital needs, capital
  expenditure, and downside cash conversion.
- **Debt structure:** maturity wall, currency, fixed/floating mix, security,
  amortization, covenants, and hedges.
- **Agency and incentives:** risk shifting, underinvestment, payout pressure,
  and management compensation.
- **Information:** issuance can signal management's view or reflect temporary
  market access.
- **Flexibility:** committed liquidity, rating objective, acquisition capacity,
  and ability to fund stress.

Target leverage should therefore be a range tested through scenarios, not the
single ratio that minimizes a spreadsheet WACC
{cite}`myers1984CapitalStructure`.

## Cost of equity

A common CAPM estimate is:

```{math}
k_e=r_f+\beta_L\mathrm{ERP}.
```

Beta measures the equity return's exposure to systematic market variation, not
total company risk {cite}`sharpe1964CapitalAssetPrices`. The estimate depends on
the market index, return frequency, sample, currency, leverage, and data
quality. For a private company or unstable issuer, analysts often unlever peer
betas and relever the central estimate:

```{math}
\beta_U
=\frac{\beta_L}
{1+(1-\tau_{\mathrm{shield}})D/E},
```

```{math}
\beta_{L,\text{target}}
=\beta_U\left[1+(1-\tau_{\mathrm{shield}})(D/E)_{\text{target}}\right].
```

These formulas assume debt beta is negligible. Material risky debt requires a
more complete asset-beta treatment. The simplified levering relation and its
assumptions should be stated rather than treated as an identity that holds under
every tax-shield or debt-risk model {cite}`hamada1972CapitalStructure`.

Do not add separate premiums for risks already reflected in cash-flow scenarios
or beta without explaining the potential double count. A discount-rate
adjustment is not a substitute for modeling a specific operational downside.

## Cost of debt

The relevant $k_d$ is the current marginal borrowing cost for the target
capital structure, currency, maturity, security, and seniority. Historical
coupon expense can be stale or distorted by issuance dates, fees, floating
rates, leases, and non-debt items.

A simple build is:

```{math}
k_d
=r_f+\text{credit spread}+\text{issuance or liquidity adjustment}.
```

When the tax benefit is expected to be usable:

```{math}
k_{d,\text{after tax}}
=k_d(1-\tau_{\mathrm{shield}}).
```

The tax adjustment does not reduce the contractual cash payment. It reflects an
expected tax consequence in valuation.

## WACC

```{math}
\mathrm{WACC}
=\frac{E}{D+E}k_e
+\frac{D}{D+E}k_d(1-\tau_{\mathrm{shield}}).
```

Include preferred stock, non-controlling interests (NCI), pensions, leases, or
other claims when they are material and align both the cash flow and capital
weights. WACC discounts FCFF,
not FCFE or dividends. Cost of equity discounts equity cash flow
{cite}`damodaran2012investment,myers1974FinancingInvestment`.

### Consistency tests

- USD nominal cash flow requires a USD nominal rate.
- Real cash flow requires a real rate.
- A project with different operating risk may need a project-specific beta or
  comparable set.
- The debt weight should reflect the target structure over the forecast, not
  an accidental balance at one date.
- The terminal WACC must be consistent with terminal leverage and mature
  business risk.

## Worked example: current structure

Assume:

- market value of equity $E=600$;
- market value of debt $D=400$;
- $\beta_L=1.20$;
- $r_f=4.0\%$;
- $\mathrm{ERP}=5.5\%$;
- $k_d=7.0\%$;
- $\tau_{\mathrm{shield}}=25.0\%$.

Cost of equity is:

```{math}
k_e=4.0\%+1.20(5.5\%)=10.60\%.
```

WACC is:

```{math}
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{WACC}
&=0.60(10.60\%)+0.40(7.00\%)(1-0.25)\\
&=8.46\%.
\end{aligned}
```

### Target-leverage case

The current unlevered beta is:

```{math}
\beta_U
=\frac{1.20}
{1+0.75(400/600)}
=0.80.
```

Assume target $D/E=0.50$ and a 6.5% marginal cost of debt at that structure.
The target levered beta is:

```{math}
\beta_{L,\text{target}}
=0.80[1+0.75(0.50)]
=1.10.
```

Target cost of equity is:

```{math}
k_{e,\text{target}}
=4.0\%+1.10(5.5\%)
=10.05\%.
```

A target $D/E=0.50$ implies equity and debt weights of two-thirds and
one-third:

```{math}
\mathrm{WACC}_{\text{target}}
=\frac{2}{3}(10.05\%)
+\frac{1}{3}(6.50\%)(1-0.25)
=8.33\%.
```

The 13-basis-point reduction from 8.46% to 8.33% is too small to establish an
optimal structure by itself. Estimation error, refinancing exposure, covenants,
and downside liquidity can dominate the apparent difference.

## When leverage changes materially: APV

A single WACC is easiest to defend when operating risk and target leverage are
reasonably stable. For a project or issuer with a changing debt schedule,
adjusted present value (APV) separates operating value from financing effects.
Use unlevered project NPV for an investment decision and unlevered enterprise
value for an issuer valuation:

```{math}
\mathrm{APV}_{\mathrm{project}}
=\mathrm{NPV}_{U,\mathrm{project}}
+\mathrm{PV}(\text{financing effects}),
\qquad
\mathrm{APV}_{\mathrm{issuer}}
=V_U+\mathrm{PV}(\text{financing effects}).
```

Financing benefits can include usable interest tax shields and genuine financing
subsidies; financing costs can include issuance, expected distress, or subsidy
recapture. Written with those signs explicitly,

```{math}
\mathrm{APV}_{\mathrm{project}}
=\mathrm{NPV}_{U,\mathrm{project}}
+\mathrm{PV}(\text{tax shields})
+\mathrm{PV}(\text{financing subsidies})
-\mathrm{PV}(\text{issuance, distress, and recapture costs}).
```

Each item needs its own risk-consistent discounting and must not also remain
embedded in the operating cash flow or discount rate
{cite}`myers1974FinancingInvestment`.

```{math}
\frac{D}{E}=\frac{w_D}{1-w_D},

w_E=1-w_D.
```

## Checks and limitations

- Beta is estimated with noise and can change with business mix and leverage.
- A risk-free rate should match cash-flow currency, not company headquarters.
- Equity risk premium estimates are model- and date-dependent.
- Historical interest expense is not a marginal debt cost.
- The interest tax shield can be delayed or lost.
- For banks, treating deposits and other operating funding as ordinary
  financing debt can make a standard FCFF/WACC setup unsuitable; an
  equity-based or institution-specific approach is often clearer
  {cite}`damodaran2012investment`.
- Small WACC changes can create large terminal-value changes; report the
  sensitivity rather than false precision.

## Practice

An issuer has market equity of USD 750m, debt of USD 250m, levered beta of 1.05,
a 3.5% USD risk-free rate, a 6.0% equity risk premium, a 6.8% pretax debt cost,
and a 25% tax rate.

1. Calculate cost of equity and current WACC.
2. Unlever beta.
3. Relever beta at target $D/E=0.50$.
4. If debt would cost 7.5% at the target, calculate target WACC.
5. Explain whether the lower or higher result alone determines target leverage.

```{dropdown} Suggested answer
Cost of equity is $3.5\%+1.05(6.0\%)=9.80\%$. Current weights are 75% equity
and 25% debt, so WACC is
$0.75(9.80\%)+0.25(6.80\%)(0.75)=8.63\%$. Unlevered beta is
$1.05/[1+0.75(250/750)]=0.84$. At target $D/E=0.50$, levered beta is
$0.84[1+0.75(0.50)]=1.155$, so cost of equity is 10.43%. Target weights are
two-thirds equity and one-third debt; target WACC is approximately
$(2/3)(10.43\%)+(1/3)(7.50\%)(0.75)=8.83\%$. Neither point alone determines
target leverage; the company must also test cash-flow stress, maturities,
covenants, tax usability, flexibility, and estimation uncertainty.
```
