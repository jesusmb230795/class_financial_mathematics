# Equity Valuation Frameworks

Module: Corporate Issuers and Equity Valuation

## Lesson summary

Valuation converts a forecast into an estimate of value under explicit
assumptions. Different methods are not independent answers when they share the
same revenue, margin, reinvestment, risk, and terminal assumptions. Their value
comes from viewing the claim from different angles and exposing inconsistencies.

This lesson develops dividend discount model (DDM), FCFF, FCFE,
residual-income, market-multiple, and sum-of-the-parts (SOTP) methods. It also
treats market efficiency and
behavioral finance as disciplines for model humility rather than reasons to
ignore either price or fundamentals. All numerical examples and comparable-
company inputs are hypothetical simulations in USD; no observed issuer or peer
data are used. Cash flows occur at year-end and discount rates are effective
annual USD rates on a nominal, inflation-inclusive basis unless stated
otherwise.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, readers should be able to:

- distinguish market price from conditional intrinsic value;
- explain the practical implications of market efficiency, anomalies, and
  behavioral biases;
- match dividends, FCFF, FCFE, and residual income with the correct claim and
  discount rate;
- bridge enterprise value to common equity value;
- select and normalize comparable-company multiples;
- construct a sum-of-the-parts valuation without double counting;
- interpret terminal-value and multiple sensitivities;
- triangulate a valuation range and state what would change it.

## Prerequisites and notation

Complete [Business Model and Forecasting](5.3.business_model_and_forecasting.md)
and [Capital Structure and WACC](5.2.capital_structure_and_wacc.md).

- $D_t$: dividend to common equity at time $t$;
- $\mathrm{FCFF}_t$: free cash flow to all capital providers;
- $\mathrm{FCFE}_t$: free cash flow to common equity after debt cash flows;
- $\mathrm{NI}_t^{\text{common}}$: net income available to common shareholders;
- $B_t$: common book value of equity;
- $\mathrm{RI}_t$: residual income;
- $\mathrm{ROE}_t^{\text{beg}}=\mathrm{NI}_t^{\text{common}}/B_{t-1}$:
  return on beginning common equity, used only for the residual-income identity
  below;
- $\tau_{\mathrm{op}}$: normalized operating cash-tax rate;
- $\tau_{\mathrm{shield}}$: usable interest-shield tax rate;
- $\mathrm{RONIC}_{N+1}$: return on new invested capital in the terminal state;
- $k_e$: cost of equity;
- $g$: perpetual nominal growth rate;
- $TV_N$: terminal value at the end of explicit period $N$.

Cash flows, rates, and terminal growth must use the same currency, inflation
convention, timing, and claim.

## Price, information, and behavior

Market efficiency does not mean every observed price equals a single true
value. It means public information and trading make persistent abnormal returns
difficult to obtain after risk, costs, and implementation. Tests of efficiency
depend jointly on a pricing model and an information set, so an apparent
anomaly can reflect risk, data mining, frictions, or mispricing
{cite}`fama1970EfficientMarkets`.

Documented return patterns are not automatic investment rules. Before using an
anomaly, specify:

- signal definition and publication date;
- universe, survivorship, delisting, and look-ahead controls;
- turnover, liquidity, tax, borrowing, and capacity costs;
- risk exposure and regime dependence;
- out-of-sample evidence.

Frequently studied anomaly hypotheses include:

| Hypothesis | Signal definition that must be fixed before testing | Main implementation concern |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Value | Price relative to book value, earnings, sales, or cash flow | Accounting comparability, distress, and slow rebalancing |
| Momentum | Relative return over a stated formation window with an explicit skip period | Turnover, reversals, crowding, and crash exposure |
| Post-announcement drift | Return after a defined earnings surprise and publication timestamp | Look-ahead bias, reporting lag, and transaction timing |
| Low-risk | Beta or volatility measured over a stated sample | Leverage, sector concentration, and benchmark choice |
| Quality or investment | Profitability, accrual, balance-sheet growth, or reinvestment rule | Multiple definitions and data revision |

These labels describe research designs, not guaranteed excess-return sources.
Testing must preserve the information set that was available at each historical
decision date. Foundational examples include value, momentum, post-earnings-
announcement drift, and accrual-related signals
{cite}`famaFrench1992CrossSection,jegadeeshTitman1993Momentum,bernardThomas1989Drift,sloan1996Accruals`.

Behavioral finance explains why decisions can depart from frictionless,
fully-rational benchmarks. Reference dependence and asymmetric treatment of
gains and losses are central to prospect theory; anchoring and other judgment
heuristics require distinct evidence
{cite}`kahnemanTversky1979ProspectTheory,tverskyKahneman1974Heuristics`.

Practical controls include:

- writing the thesis and falsification test before seeing the final valuation;
- using base rates and an outside view;
- separating facts from estimates;
- requiring a downside case;
- recording the current market-implied expectation;
- assigning an independent reviewer to challenge the terminal assumptions.

## Claim and rate consistency

| Valuation input | Claim valued | Discount rate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dividends | Distributed common-equity cash | Cost of equity |
| FCFE | Common-equity cash after investment and net borrowing | Cost of equity |
| FCFF | Operating cash available before debt cash flows | WACC |
| Residual income | Common-equity book value plus excess accounting return | Cost of equity |
| Equity multiple | Common equity | Matched equity metric |
| Enterprise multiple | Operations available to capital providers | Matched pre-financing metric |

Discounting FCFF at cost of equity and then subtracting debt mismatches the
claim and required return. Discounting FCFE at WACC has the symmetric defect.
Either mismatch biases value; its direction is not universal.

## Dividend discount models

For a stable company with next-period dividend $D_1$, perpetual nominal
growth $g$, and $k_e>g$:

```{math}
V_0=\frac{D_1}{k_e-g}.
```

If the assumed current dividend is USD 1.20 per share, growth is 4%, and cost of
equity is 10%:

```{math}
D_1=1.20(1.04)=1.248,
```

```{math}
V_0=\frac{1.248}{0.10-0.04}=20.80.
```

The constant-growth model is appropriate only when payout, leverage, growth,
and return on equity are sustainable. A multi-stage DDM forecasts dividends
during a transition and applies a stable formula only after those conditions
are met {cite}`gordonShapiro1956CapitalEquipment`.

Dividend policy can make DDM less informative when distributions are far below
capacity or temporarily debt-funded. In those cases, FCFE or residual income
can provide a better economic cross-check.

## FCFF valuation

```{math}
\mathrm{FCFF}
=\mathrm{EBIT}(1-\tau_{\mathrm{op}})
+\text{D\&A}
-\text{Capital expenditures}
-\Delta\text{Operating working capital}.
```

For an explicit horizon:

```{math}
\text{Enterprise value}_0
=\sum_{t=1}^{N}\frac{\mathrm{FCFF}_t}{(1+\mathrm{WACC})^t}
+\frac{TV_N}{(1+\mathrm{WACC})^N}.
```

With a perpetual-growth terminal value:

```{math}
TV_N
=\frac{\mathrm{FCFF}_{N+1}}{\mathrm{WACC}-g}
=\frac{\mathrm{FCFF}_N(1+g)}{\mathrm{WACC}-g}.
```

The terminal year must represent a sustainable operating state. Growth,
reinvestment, ROIC, margins, leverage, tax, and WACC should be mutually
consistent. With a positive, stable return on new invested capital, the
steady-state reinvestment check is:

```{math}
\text{Reinvestment rate}_{N+1}=\frac{g}{\mathrm{RONIC}_{N+1}},
```

```{math}
\mathrm{FCFF}_{N+1}
=\mathrm{NOPAT}_{N+1}
\left(1-\frac{g}{\mathrm{RONIC}_{N+1}}\right).
```

These identities are diagnostics, not permission to force a growth rate. The
RONIC definition, invested-capital perimeter, inflation convention, and timing
must match the forecast. Require $\mathrm{WACC}>g$ and
$0\le g/\mathrm{RONIC}_{N+1}\le1$ in a stable positive-growth case
{cite}`damodaran2012investment`.

### Worked example

Forecast FCFF is USD 80m, USD 88m, and USD 95m in Years 1–3. WACC is 9% and
perpetual growth is 3%.

```{math}
TV_3
=\frac{95(1.03)}{0.09-0.03}
=1{,}630.83.
```

The present value of explicit FCFF is USD 220.82m and the present value of
terminal value is USD 1,259.30m:

```{math}
\text{Enterprise value}=1{,}480.12.
```

Assume debt of USD 400m, non-controlling interests (NCI) of USD 20m, excess cash
of USD 80m, and 100m diluted shares:

```{math}
\text{Common equity value}
=1{,}480.12-400-20+80
=1{,}140.12,
```

```{math}
\text{Value per diluted share}
=\frac{1{,}140.12}{100}
=11.40.
```

Terminal value represents 85.1% of enterprise value. The model is therefore
primarily a terminal-economics estimate, despite three explicit forecast years.

## FCFE valuation

One common bridge is:

```{math}
\mathrm{FCFE}
=\text{Net income available to common}
+\text{D\&A}
-\text{Capital expenditures}
-\Delta\text{Operating working capital}
+\text{Net borrowing}.
```

With aligned working-capital, capital-expenditure, claim, and cash-tax
definitions—and the simplifying assumption that operating and interest-shield
tax rates reconcile—the enterprise and equity cash-flow paths satisfy:

```{math}
\mathrm{FCFE}_t=\mathrm{FCFF}_t
-\text{Interest}_t(1-\tau_{\mathrm{shield},t})
+\text{Net borrowing}_t.
```

FCFE is discounted at $k_e$. It is useful when debt policy can be forecast
explicitly. It becomes unstable when leverage is changing sharply or debt is
used as an unexplained cash plug. FCFF is often clearer in those cases because
it values operations before financing and applies an explicit equity bridge
{cite}`damodaran2012investment`. If leverage and tax shields change materially,
use period-consistent rates or the APV separation introduced in the prior
lesson; do not force a single constant WACC onto an incompatible debt path
{cite}`myers1974FinancingInvestment`.

## Residual-income valuation

Residual income measures accounting earnings after a charge for beginning
common equity:

```{math}
\mathrm{RI}_t=\mathrm{NI}_t^{\text{common}}-k_eB_{t-1}.
```

Under a clean-surplus relationship and consistent accounting:

```{math}
V_0
=B_0+\sum_{t=1}^{N}\frac{\mathrm{RI}_t}{(1+k_e)^t}
+\frac{TV^{\mathrm{RI}}_N}{(1+k_e)^N}.
```

If residual income enters a defensible constant-growth state with
$k_e>g_{\mathrm{RI}}$, one explicit continuation is:

```{math}
TV_N^{\mathrm{RI}}
=\frac{\mathrm{RI}_{N+1}}{k_e-g_{\mathrm{RI}}}.
```

Residual income can be useful when dividends or FCFE are temporarily negative
but book value and accounting earnings are informative. It requires adjustments
for share transactions, other comprehensive income, write-offs, acquisitions,
and accounting policies that break comparability.

A company creates positive residual income when return on **beginning** common
equity exceeds $k_e$:

```{math}
\mathrm{RI}_t=(\mathrm{ROE}_t^{\text{beg}}-k_e)B_{t-1}.
```

This identity must not use Module 4's average-equity ROE, which answers a
different performance question. The valuation also requires a clean-surplus
roll-forward after common-owner transactions and comprehensive-income items are
handled consistently {cite}`ohlson1995EarningsBookValues`. Competitive fade is
a modeling prior to test: durable evidence may support persistence, while weak
advantages require faster convergence.

## Market multiples

Common measures include:

| Multiple | Numerator | Denominator | Main alignment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Price/earnings (P/E) | Common equity value | Net income to common | Share count, tax, leverage, unusual items |
| Price/book (P/B) | Common equity value | Common book value | Asset recognition, write-offs, ROE |
| EV/revenue | Enterprise value | Revenue | Margin and capital-intensity differences |
| EV/EBITDA | Enterprise value | EBITDA | Lease, stock compensation, capital needs |
| EV/EBIT | Enterprise value | EBIT | Depreciation and asset-age comparability |

Peer selection should use business model, geography, customer, growth, margins,
capital intensity, risk, and accounting—not merely sector labels. Use the same
market date and forward or historical period across numerator and denominator.
Show the full distribution, identify outliers before seeing the desired answer,
and apply a justified subject-company positioning
{cite}`liuNissimThomas2002Multiples,bhojrajLee2002Peer`.

In the FCFF example, suppose a hypothetical comparable set supports 8.0×
next-year EV/EBITDA and simulated subject EBITDA is USD 180m:

```{math}
\text{Enterprise value}=8.0\times180=1{,}440.
```

Using the same debt, NCI, cash, and shares:

```{math}
\text{Value per share}
=\frac{1{,}440-400-20+80}{100}
=11.00.
```

The multiple cross-check is close to the USD 11.40 DCF result, but it is not
independent if peer prices embed similar rate and growth expectations.

## Sum-of-the-parts valuation

Sum-of-the-parts (SOTP) valuation is appropriate when segments have different
economics, capital structures, or natural peer groups. First label each segment
method as enterprise-value or equity-value based. Either normalize all segments
to enterprise value before one consolidated claim bridge, or maintain an
explicit claim matrix for mixed methods. For an enterprise-value SOTP:

1. normalize segment revenue, earnings, capital, and cash flow;
2. select an enterprise DCF or enterprise multiple consistent with that segment;
3. value corporate costs and shared assets;
4. subtract debt, pensions, preferred claims, NCI, and contingent obligations;
5. add excess cash, investments, tax assets, or other non-operating value;
6. reconcile intersegment eliminations and ownership percentages.

Do not apply a whole-company multiple after separately valuing the segments.
Do not subtract corporate cost both from segment cash flow and as a separate
capitalized item. A holding-company discount should represent identified tax,
cost, governance, or liquidity effects rather than serve as a balancing plug.
If a segment is valued directly as equity, do not subtract its assigned debt a
second time in the consolidated bridge.

For a mixed-method SOTP, label enterprise-valued segments $j$, equity-valued
segments $k$, the present value of unallocated corporate costs $C_0$, other
non-operating assets $A_0$, and only the claims not already reflected in a
segment equity value $L_0^{\mathrm{unassigned}}$:

```{math}
E_0^{\mathrm{common}}
=\sum_j \mathrm{EV}_{j,0}
+\sum_k E_{k,0}
-C_0+A_0-L_0^{\mathrm{unassigned}}.
```

For example, suppose Segment A has enterprise value of USD 900m; Segment B has
common-equity value of USD 260m after its assigned debt; unallocated corporate
costs have a present value of USD 80m; an investment is worth USD 40m; and the
remaining group bridge contains USD 300m of debt, USD 20m of NCI, and USD 60m
of excess cash. The common-equity value is

```{math}
900+260-80+40-300-20+60=860\text{ USD m}.
```

With 100m diluted shares, the mixed SOTP gives USD 8.60 per share. The assigned
debt already embedded in Segment B's equity value is excluded from the USD
300m group debt amount.

## Method selection

| Situation | Primary method | Cross-check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stable dividend capacity and payout | Multi-stage DDM | FCFE, P/E |
| Non-financial company with stable target leverage | FCFF | Multiples, residual income |
| Material, forecastable leverage changes | APV or period-specific FCFF discounting | FCFE, financing bridge |
| Predictable debt policy | FCFE | FCFF |
| Informative book value and temporary cash-flow distortion | Residual income | P/B, FCFE |
| Distinct operating segments | SOTP | Whole-company DCF |
| Bank or insurer | Equity cash flow or residual income | P/B, P/E, institution-specific metrics |

Triangulation does not mean averaging every method. Weight the method whose
claim, accounting, and forecast fit the company, and use others to diagnose
differences.

## Checks and limitations

- $g$ must be below the discount rate and sustainable for the relevant
  currency and economy.
- Terminal margin and ROIC require a competitive rationale.
- Enterprise-to-equity bridges frequently omit leases, pensions, NCI, options,
  associates, or restricted cash.
- Multiples transfer the market's errors and require aligned definitions.
- Residual income is sensitive to accounting quality and clean-surplus breaks.
- Per-share value requires a diluted share count consistent with options,
  convertibles, and repurchases.
- A valuation range is conditional, not a guarantee or a price target detached
  from its as-of date.

## Practice

1. A stable issuer paid a USD 0.80 dividend. If growth is 3% and cost of equity
   is 9%, calculate constant-growth DDM value.
2. Another company has FCFF of USD 50m in Year 1 and USD 55m in Year 2. Use an
   8% WACC and 2.5% perpetual growth after Year 2. Calculate enterprise value.
3. Bridge to common equity using debt of USD 200m, NCI of USD 10m, excess cash
   of USD 30m, and 50m diluted shares.
4. State two facts needed before accepting the terminal value.

```{dropdown} Suggested answer
Next dividend is $0.80(1.03)=0.824$, so DDM value is
$0.824/(0.09-0.03)=13.73$ USD per share. Terminal value at Year 2 is
$55(1.025)/(0.08-0.025)=1{,}025.00$ USD m. Discounted FCFF and terminal value
produce enterprise value of approximately USD 972.22m. Common equity is
$972.22-200-10+30=792.22$ USD m, or USD 15.84 per diluted share. Acceptance
requires evidence that 2.5% nominal growth, terminal margin, reinvestment,
ROIC, leverage, tax, and 8% WACC describe a sustainable mature state.
```
