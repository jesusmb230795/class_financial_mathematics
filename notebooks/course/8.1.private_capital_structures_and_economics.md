# Private Capital Structures and Fund Economics

Module: Alternative Investments

## Lesson summary

Private capital includes venture capital, growth equity, buyouts, private
credit, and direct or co-investment structures. Their operating risks differ,
but an investor in a closed-end fund usually experiences the same basic
sequence: commitment, capital calls, investment, realization, distribution,
and residual NAV. This lesson converts that sequence into an auditable
investor cash-flow ledger and shows how fees and a simplified waterfall change
gross asset proceeds into net investor performance.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, readers should be able to:

- distinguish venture capital, growth equity, buyouts, and private debt by
  stage, claim, control, leverage, and cash-flow pattern;
- explain limited-partner, general-partner, commitment, capital-call,
  distribution, fund-life, and co-investment mechanics;
- calculate management fees, a preferred return, carried interest, DPI, RVPI,
  TVPI, and a simple net IRR;
- distinguish whole-fund from deal-by-deal waterfalls and identify clawback
  risk;
- evaluate whether a reported return is gross or net and whether financing has
  changed its timing.

## Prerequisites and notation

Complete the [Module 8 overview](../../chapters/08-alternative-investments.md).
The lesson uses:

- \(K\): committed capital;
- \(C_t\): capital contributed by the limited partner at date \(t\);
- \(D_t\): cash distributed to the limited partner at date \(t\);
- \(NAV_t\): reported residual net asset value;
- \(m\): annual management-fee rate;
- \(h\): preferred-return or hurdle rate;
- \(c\): carried-interest rate.

All rates are decimals in calculations. Cash flows are MXN millions unless
another currency and unit are stated. Positive values received by the investor
are distributions; contributions are reported separately rather than hidden
by a sign convention.

## Private-capital strategies

| Strategy | Typical asset state | Primary value-creation claim | Central risks |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Venture capital | early product or market development, often negative cash flow | product adoption, scale, follow-on financing, exit | technical failure, dilution, binary outcomes, long duration |
| Growth equity | established revenue, expansion capital, minority or shared control | market expansion, operating leverage, professionalization | entry multiple, governance rights, execution |
| Buyout | mature company, usually control, often acquisition debt | operational improvement, deleveraging, strategic repositioning | leverage, cyclicality, refinancing, exit multiple |
| Private debt | contractual senior, subordinated, unitranche, or asset-backed claim | coupon, fees, collateral, covenants, recovery | default, recovery, documentation, illiquidity |
| Co-investment | direct participation alongside a lead sponsor | lower fee load and targeted exposure | selection bias, concentration, compressed diligence |

These are economic descriptions, not guarantees. A "growth" deal can use
leverage, a "private debt" instrument can have equity-like loss, and a
co-investment can carry governance obligations. The controlling agreements
determine the claim {cite}`damodaran2012investment,ilpaPrinciples2019`.

## Fund structure and cash-flow ledger

A common closed-end structure separates:

1. the **limited partners (LPs)** that commit capital;
2. the **general partner (GP)** that controls the fund;
3. the **management company** that receives management fees;
4. the **portfolio vehicles** that hold individual investments.

A commitment is not an immediate investment. It is a maximum contractual
funding obligation subject to the agreement. The analyst therefore tracks at
least:

| Date | Event | Contribution | Distribution | NAV | Unfunded commitment |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| \(t_0\) | Subscription | 0 | 0 | 0 | \(K\) |
| \(t_1\) | Capital call | \(C_1\) | 0 | manager mark | \(K-C_1\) |
| \(t_2\) | Partial realization | 0 | \(D_2\) | manager mark | remaining obligation |
| \(T\) | Final realization | 0 | \(D_T\) | 0 | 0 |

The cash-flow dates must be actual dates for an XIRR-style calculation. A
quarter-end approximation can materially change short-horizon performance.

### Management fees

The fee base matters as much as the fee rate. A simplified annual fee is:

```{math}
Fee_t=m\times Base_t.
```

The base may be committed capital during the investment period and invested
capital, acquisition cost, or NAV later. A step-down therefore cannot be
modeled by multiplying one rate by original commitment for the full fund life.
Organizational expenses, broken-deal costs, monitoring fees, offsets, and
fund-level financing must be recorded separately if they are material.
Institutional reporting guidance treats those fee and expense categories as
distinct inputs rather than burying them in a single net-return number
{cite}`ilpaPrinciples2019`.

For example, a MXN 100m commitment charged 2% annually on commitment for three
years incurs MXN 2m per year before other expenses. If year-four terms step
down to 1.5% on MXN 60m of remaining invested cost, that year's fee is:

```{math}
Fee_4=0.015\times60=\text{MXN }0.9\text{m},
```

not MXN 2m. The fee schedule must be modeled period by period.

### Carried interest and waterfalls

A distribution waterfall states who receives each peso and in what order. A
simplified whole-fund waterfall without a GP catch-up is:

1. return LP contributed capital;
2. pay the LP preferred return;
3. split remaining profit between LP and GP at \(1-c\) and \(c\).

A **whole-fund** or European-style waterfall generally measures aggregate
fund economics before carry is paid. A **deal-by-deal** or American-style
waterfall can pay carry after individual realizations. The latter creates
greater clawback risk if early wins are followed by later losses. Actual
agreements can include catch-ups, escrow, tax distributions, recycling, and
different treatment by investor; the legal text controls.
Fund-level transparency, governance, and alignment should be evaluated against
the actual agreement and side letters, using institutional principles as a
review framework rather than as a substitute for the contract
{cite}`ilpaPrinciples2019`.

For one contribution $C$ held for $\tau$ years, the accrued preference is
either simple or compounded according to the agreement:

```{math}
H_{\mathrm{simple}}=Ch\tau,
\qquad
H_{\mathrm{compound}}=C\left[(1+h)^\tau-1\right].
```

In the simplified no-catch-up case, if $P$ is distributable proceeds, the
residual profit and GP carry are:

```{math}
R=\max\left(0,P-C-H\right),
\qquad
Carry_{GP}=cR,
\qquad
Distribution_{LP}=\min(P,C+H)+(1-c)R.
```

These formulas describe the classroom sequence only; they do not override the
governing waterfall, escrow, clawback, recycling, or tax provisions.

## Worked example: investor-level waterfall

Assume one LP contributes MXN 12m at \(t=0\). The contribution includes all
investment capital and fees for this simplified case. After three years, the
fund has MXN 20m available for final distribution. The agreement uses:

- simple preferred return \(h=8\%\) per year for this classroom calculation;
- whole-fund waterfall;
- no GP catch-up;
- carried interest \(c=20\%\) on residual profit after the preference;
- no taxes, recycling, interim cash flows, or clawback adjustment.

The preferred return is:

```{math}
Preference=12\times0.08\times3=\text{MXN }2.88\text{m}.
```

The distribution layers are:

| Layer | LP | GP |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Return of capital | 12.000 | 0 |
| Preferred return | 2.880 | 0 |
| Residual profit: \(20-12-2.88=5.12\) | 4.096 | 1.024 |
| **Total** | **18.976** | **1.024** |

The LP's net multiple and annualized return are:

```{math}
MOIC_{\text{LP}}=\frac{18.976}{12}=1.5813\times,
```

```{math}
IRR_{\text{LP}}=
\left(\frac{18.976}{12}\right)^{1/3}-1
=16.50\%.
```

The second formula is valid only because this example has one initial
contribution and one terminal distribution. An actual fund IRR must use every
dated contribution and distribution.

If the MXN 20m were described as "gross proceeds," the investor could not use
the gross \(1.67\times\) multiple as net performance. The waterfall reduces the
LP result even before taxes or other expenses.

## Performance measures and interpretation

At the measurement date:

```{math}
DPI=\frac{\sum D_t}{\sum C_t},
\qquad
RVPI=\frac{NAV_T}{\sum C_t},
\qquad
TVPI=DPI+RVPI.
```

- DPI is realized value relative to paid-in capital.
- RVPI depends on the current valuation policy.
- TVPI combines realized and unrealized value.
- IRR incorporates timing but can be affected by early distributions and
  subscription-line financing.
- A public-market-equivalent comparison should use the same dated cash flows,
  a declared investable public benchmark, and a documented reinvestment rule.

No single measure resolves manager selection. Report at least one timing-aware
measure, one multiple, the realized/unrealized split, and a benchmark
comparison.
ILPA reporting resources are useful consistency frameworks for the underlying
cash-flow, fee, and gross/net distinctions, but the fund's governing documents
and complete investor ledger remain controlling evidence
{cite}`ilpaPrinciples2019`.

## Co-investments and private debt

Co-investments can reduce the blended fee rate, but they increase deal
concentration and require rapid diligence. The analyst should compare:

- allocation policy and conflicts among fund, GP, and co-investors;
- fees and carry charged at both fund and vehicle level;
- governance, information, follow-on funding, and exit rights;
- whether the co-investment duplicates a concentrated fund exposure.

Private debt adds contractual evidence: principal, currency, coupon and
compounding, reference rate, floor, maturity, amortization, collateral,
seniority, covenants, call protection, default definition, recovery process,
and transfer restrictions. A high stated yield is not a return forecast until
expected default, recovery, fees, prepayment, and workout timing are modeled.
The discounting, credit, recovery, and contractual-cash-flow foundations are
developed in the fixed-income literature {cite}`fabozzi2019foundations`.

## Checks and limitations

- Never infer available capital from commitment alone; subtract paid-in and
  document recycling provisions.
- Do not compare gross manager returns with net investor returns.
- Confirm whether management fees are inside or outside reported paid-in
  capital.
- Record whether an IRR uses actual dates, quarter ends, or a subscription
  facility-adjusted cash-flow history.
- Treat residual NAV as an estimate subject to valuation lag and policy.
- Read side letters and the governing agreement; a marketing deck does not
  define the waterfall.
- The worked example uses simple preference and no catch-up. It is not a model
  of every market convention.

## Practice

A simulated LP contributes MXN 15m at inception. Four years later, the fund
distributes MXN 27m. Use a whole-fund waterfall with a 7% simple annual
preferred return, no catch-up, and 20% carry on remaining profit.

1. Calculate the preference.
2. Allocate the distribution between LP and GP.
3. Calculate the LP net MOIC and annualized return.
4. Name two agreement terms that could make the actual result differ.

```{dropdown} Suggested answer
The preference is \(15\times0.07\times4=\) MXN 4.2m. After returning MXN 15m
and paying the preference, residual profit is \(27-15-4.2=\) MXN 7.8m. The LP
receives 80%, or MXN 6.24m, and the GP receives MXN 1.56m. Total LP proceeds
are MXN 25.44m, so net MOIC is \(25.44/15=1.696\times\) and annualized return
is \((25.44/15)^{1/4}-1=14.12\%\). A catch-up, compounding preference,
deal-by-deal carry, interim cash flows, escrow, recycling, taxes, or additional
fees could change the result.
```
