# Hedge Funds, Digital Assets, and Liquidity Risk

Module: Alternative Investments

## Lesson summary

Hedge-fund and digital-asset labels reveal little about the losses an investor
can experience. A strategy must be decomposed into long and short exposures,
factor sensitivities, leverage, financing, counterparties, liquidity terms,
valuation sources, and operational controls. Digital ownership adds custody,
protocol, oracle, and transfer risks that can exist even when the underlying
economic claim resembles a familiar security.

This lesson builds an exposure-and-liquidity map, converts gross returns to
after-fee performance, and adds an explicit liquidation cost to a
positive-loss risk scenario.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, readers should be able to:

- distinguish major hedge-fund strategy families by their economic exposures;
- calculate gross and net exposure and explain why neither is a complete risk
  measure;
- interpret leverage, financing, short-sale, counterparty, and crowded-exit
  risks;
- compare lockups, notice periods, gates, side pockets, and suspension terms;
- identify custody, smart-contract, oracle, protocol, settlement, and
  regulatory risks in digital assets and tokenized claims;
- explain how stale pricing can suppress measured volatility and correlation;
- calculate an after-fee return and a simple liquidity-adjusted positive loss.

## Prerequisites and notation

Complete [Real Assets and Commodities](8.3.real_assets_and_commodities.md). The
lesson uses:

- \(NAV\): investor or fund net asset value;
- \(L\): market value of long positions;
- \(S\): absolute market value of short positions;
- \(E_g=(L+S)/NAV\): gross exposure;
- \(E_n=(L-S)/NAV\): net exposure;
- \(R_g\): gross investment return before manager fees;
- \(VaR_\alpha\): non-negative loss magnitude at lower-tail probability
  \(\alpha\).

Amounts are MXN millions. Exposures are reported as a percentage of current
NAV. Derivative notional and delta-adjusted exposure must be labeled
separately.

## Strategy families and exposure questions

| Strategy family | Common economic claim | Exposure questions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Equity long/short | security selection with partial market hedge | beta, sector, size, style, net and gross, short borrow |
| Equity market neutral | relative security returns with low intended beta | residual beta, factor neutrality, crowding, financing |
| Event driven | merger, restructuring, catalyst, or distressed payoff | deal break, recovery, legal timing, position asymmetry |
| Relative value | convergence between related instruments | basis, funding, model, leverage, forced unwind |
| Global macro | directional or relative rates, FX, equity, commodity views | notional, option convexity, correlation regime |
| Managed futures | systematic trend or other rules across futures | signal horizon, volatility scaling, roll, whipsaw |
| Credit | spread, capital-structure, or default/recovery view | jump-to-default, liquidity, seniority, hedge basis |
| Multi-strategy | capital allocation across internal teams | hidden overlap, central leverage, liquidity mismatch |

A return regression can estimate exposures to observed factors, but a small
beta is not proof of market neutrality. Options, regime shifts, stale marks,
and changing positions can create nonlinear or time-varying exposure.

## Leverage and financing

For a fund with MXN 160m long, MXN 70m short, and MXN 100m NAV:

```{math}
E_g=\frac{160+70}{100}=230\%,
```

```{math}
E_n=\frac{160-70}{100}=90\%.
```

The net exposure suggests a directional long bias; the gross exposure shows
how much market value may need to be financed or unwound. Neither captures:

- derivative convexity or path-dependent exposure;
- offsetting positions that cease to offset under stress;
- margin calls and variation margin timing;
- short recalls, borrow cost, or unlimited short-side loss;
- counterparty netting and collateral eligibility;
- liquidity concentration or common positioning.

Leverage reports should state whether they use gross market value, accounting
assets/equity, risk-weighted exposure, derivative notional, or a
delta-equivalent measure.

## Liquidity terms and mismatch

### Investor-level liquidity

| Term | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Lockup | When can the investor first request redemption? |
| Notice period | How far in advance must the request be submitted? |
| Redemption frequency | Daily, monthly, quarterly, annual, or event-based? |
| Gate | What percentage of fund or investor NAV can be redeemed at once? |
| Side pocket | Which assets are excluded from ordinary redemption and how are they valued? |
| Suspension | Who can stop valuation, subscription, or redemption, and under what conditions? |
| Settlement | When does cash arrive after the redemption date? |

### Asset-level liquidity

The same fund may promise quarterly redemptions while holding instruments that
require months to sell. The analysis compares investor redemption terms with
position-level days-to-liquidate under normal and stressed volume, spread, and
market-impact assumptions. A gate can protect remaining investors from a fire
sale, but it also transfers timing risk to the redeeming investor.

## Stale pricing and reported risk

Infrequent appraisal or manager marks can smooth observed returns. A simple
illustration is:

```{math}
r_t^{obs}=(1-\lambda)r_t^{economic}
+\lambda r_{t-1}^{obs},
\qquad 0\leq\lambda<1.
```

A higher \(\lambda\) can lower observed volatility, delay recognition of a
shock, and alter observed correlations. This equation is a diagnostic
illustration, not a universal estimator. "Unsmoothing" requires a defensible
model and can amplify noise.

Risk reporting should supplement return volatility with:

- valuation age and source distribution;
- drawdown and recovery duration;
- gross and net exposure;
- stress losses by factor and position;
- liquidation horizon and concentration;
- financing and collateral calls;
- counterparty and redemption scenarios.

Positive-loss VaR and Expected Shortfall retain the book's sign convention.
They are not maximum losses and should not be annualized mechanically
{cite}`jorion2007var,mcneil2015quantitative`.

## After-fee performance

Suppose an investor begins with MXN 100m and the strategy earns a 12% gross
return during one year. A simplified contract charges a 2% management fee on
beginning NAV and a 20% incentive fee on profit after the management fee,
with no hurdle, high-water-mark issue, or other expense:

```{math}
\text{Management fee}=0.02\times100=\text{MXN }2\text{m},
```

```{math}
\text{Incentive fee}=0.20\times(12-2)=\text{MXN }2\text{m}.
```

Ending investor value is MXN 108m and the simplified net return is 8%. Actual
agreements can calculate fees monthly or quarterly, crystallize incentive fees
at different dates, include hurdles or high-water marks, and charge fund
expenses. The cash-flow schedule controls.

## Worked example: liquidity-adjusted loss

Return to the fund with MXN 100m NAV, 230% gross exposure, and 90% net
exposure. A ten-business-day model reports a 99% confidence VaR of MXN 5.5m as
a **positive loss magnitude**. A stressed liquidation schedule estimates:

- bid-ask spread cost: MXN 0.3m;
- market impact: MXN 0.6m;
- financing and delay cost: MXN 0.3m.

Under an additive classroom convention:

```{math}
C_{\text{liquidation}}=0.3+0.6+0.3
=\text{MXN }1.2\text{m},
```

```{math}
LVaR_{1\%}=5.5+1.2
=\text{MXN }6.7\text{m}=6.7\%\text{ of NAV}.
```

This is not a universal liquidity-risk model. The analyst must confirm that
the market-impact scenario is not already embedded in the return history and
must also show a loss beyond VaR, such as an Expected Shortfall or named stress
scenario.

## Digital assets and tokenization

A digital-asset position card separates:

| Layer | Evidence |
| --- | --- |
| Economic claim | cash flow, governance right, collateral claim, usage right, or no contractual cash flow |
| Token and network | contract address, chain, supply rule, upgrade authority, consensus or validation mechanism |
| Custody | legal owner, key control, recovery, segregation, insurance limitations |
| Trading | venue, quote currency, volume, spread, market fragmentation, settlement |
| Protocol | smart-contract code, oracle, bridge, validator, stable-value, and liquidation dependencies |
| Counterparty | exchange, custodian, issuer, lender, market maker, and collateral terms |
| Legal and tax | jurisdiction-specific classification and restrictions at the analysis date |
| Valuation | observed price sources, timestamp, cross-venue reconciliation, model assumption |

Tokenization changes the record and transfer mechanism; it does not by itself
create enforceable ownership, reliable valuation, or liquidity. If a token
represents a conventional asset, reconcile token supply and legal entitlement
to the off-chain asset. If it has no contractual cash flow, do not force a DCF
claim; document the observable usage, scarcity, network, and market assumptions
and their limitations. Tokenized monetary and financial arrangements also
depend on the relationship among the ledger, settlement asset, governance, and
legal claim {cite}`bisTokenisation2023`.

## Checks and limitations

- Strategy names are not exposure evidence.
- Report gross, net, derivative, factor, and stress exposures separately.
- Do not equate low reported volatility with low economic risk when marks are
  stale.
- Reconcile asset liquidity with investor redemption promises.
- Confirm high-water-mark, hurdle, fee base, crystallization, and fund expenses
  before calculating net performance.
- Treat gates and side pockets as contractual states, not rare footnotes.
- Verify digital-asset ownership, custody, code, and legal claim independently.
- Never infer liquidity from quoted volume on one venue.

## Practice

A simulated fund has MXN 250m NAV, MXN 325m long positions, MXN 125m short
positions, and a five-day 95% confidence VaR of MXN 11m. Stressed spread,
impact, and financing costs are MXN 0.8m, MXN 1.7m, and MXN 0.5m.

1. Calculate gross and net exposure.
2. Calculate additive liquidity-adjusted VaR in MXN and as a percentage of NAV.
3. State two reasons this figure can understate an actual redemption loss.
4. Name one investor-level and one asset-level liquidity control.

```{dropdown} Suggested answer
Gross exposure is \((325+125)/250=180\%\). Net exposure is
\((325-125)/250=80\%\). Liquidation cost is MXN 3.0m, so additive LVaR is MXN
14.0m, or 5.6% of NAV. It can understate loss if factor correlations jump,
positions gap before liquidation, market depth disappears, counterparties
raise margin, or gates delay proceeds. An investor-level control is a maximum
notice-plus-settlement period or gate limit; an asset-level control is a
stressed days-to-liquidate or position-size limit.
```
