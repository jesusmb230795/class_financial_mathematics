# Ratios, DuPont, and Capital Efficiency

Module: Financial Statement Analysis and Financial Modeling

## Lesson summary

Ratios compress financial statements into questions about margins, asset use,
capital structure, liquidity, and debt service. Compression is useful only when
definitions remain visible. The same label can use average or ending balances,
gross or net debt, reported or normalized earnings, and different lease or
noncontrolling-interest treatments.

This lesson builds a ratio tree from the reconciled simulation in the previous
lesson. It separates shareholder return on equity from operating return on
invested capital and shows how working-capital days connect ratios to forecast
drivers.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, readers should be able to:

- calculate profitability, liquidity, leverage, solvency, and coverage ratios;
- decompose ROE with the three-part DuPont identity;
- calculate ROIC using a documented NOPAT and invested-capital definition;
- distinguish operating performance from financing effects;
- translate receivable, inventory, and payable balances into operating days;
- identify denominator, period, and sector choices that limit comparison.

## Prerequisites and notation

Complete [Analysis Framework and Statement Linkages](4.1.analysis_framework_and_statement_linkages.md).
The author-created simulation uses USD millions and a 365-day fiscal year; it
does not represent a real issuer. Multiples are displayed with the symbol
$\times$, not the letter $x$.

For a flow $X$ and balance $B$, use average balance
$\overline{B}=(B_{t-1}+B_t)/2$ unless the lesson explicitly labels an
ending-balance approximation. $T$ is the normalized marginal cash tax rate.
EBITDA is EBIT plus depreciation and amortization only when those components
are measured consistently. The simulation has no preferred equity or
noncontrolling interest, so total equity and common equity are the same; real
analyses must bridge those claims explicitly.

## Build a ratio tree

Ratios answer different questions:

| Family | Question | Illustrative measures |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Profitability | How much profit does each sales unit produce? | Gross, EBIT, and net margins |
| Efficiency | How much revenue does the asset base support? | Asset turnover, working-capital days |
| Capital return | What return is earned on shareholder or operating capital? | ROE, ROIC |
| Liquidity | Can near-term obligations be met with near-term resources? | Current and quick ratios |
| Solvency | How large are financial claims relative to earnings or capital? | Debt/equity, net debt/EBITDA |
| Coverage | How much operating profit is available for contractual charges? | EBIT/interest, fixed-charge coverage |

A ratio should be presented with its formula, period, unit, source balances, and
adjustments. "Leverage increased" is incomplete if the reader cannot tell
whether leverage means debt/equity, debt/assets, or net debt/EBITDA.
Ratio decomposition organizes questions; it does not by itself establish
causality or forecastability. Research on margin, turnover, and reformulated
financial statements provides evidence for a structured analysis while also
showing why changes in drivers matter more than a static identity
{cite}`nissimPenman2001RatioAnalysis,fairfieldYohn2001profitability`.

## Profitability and operating efficiency

Using revenue of USD 1,000m, COGS of USD 600m, EBIT of USD 150m, and net income
of USD 90m:

```{math}
\text{Gross margin}
=\frac{\text{Revenue}-\mathrm{COGS}}{\text{Revenue}}
=\frac{1{,}000-600}{1{,}000}
=40.0\%,
```

```{math}
\text{EBIT margin}=\frac{150}{1{,}000}=15.0\%,
```

```{math}
\text{Net margin}=\frac{90}{1{,}000}=9.0\%.
```

Average total assets are:

```{math}
\overline{\text{Assets}}=\frac{740+790}{2}=765.
```

Asset turnover is:

```{math}
\text{Asset turnover}
=\frac{\text{Revenue}}{\overline{\text{Assets}}}
=\frac{1{,}000}{765}
=1.307\times.
```

Margins and turnover should be read together. A low-margin distributor can
earn an attractive capital return through rapid turnover, while a high-margin
infrastructure business can require a large asset base.

## DuPont analysis and ROE

Return on equity is:

```{math}
\text{ROE}
=\frac{\text{Net income available to common shareholders}}
{\text{Average common equity}}.
```

Average equity is $(390+455)/2=422.5$, so:

```{math}
\text{ROE}=\frac{90}{422.5}=21.30\%.
```

The three-part DuPont identity decomposes ROE:

```{math}
\text{ROE}
=
\underbrace{\frac{\text{Net income available to common}}{\text{Revenue}}}_{\text{net margin}}
\times
\underbrace{\frac{\text{Revenue}}{\text{Average assets}}}_{\text{asset turnover}}
\times
\underbrace{\frac{\text{Average assets}}{\text{Average equity}}}_{\text{equity multiplier}}.
```

For the simulation:

```{math}
9.00\%\times1.307\times1.811=21.30\%.
```

This decomposition prevents a high ROE from being interpreted automatically as
superior operations. ROE can rise through higher margins, greater asset
efficiency, or a larger equity multiplier. The last mechanism usually means
more leverage or a smaller equity base and can increase financial risk.

Repurchases, accumulated losses, or write-downs can make average common equity
very small or negative. In those cases, ROE can become unstable or
economically meaningless.

## ROIC and the operating view

ROIC asks whether operating assets earn an adequate return independent of the
current mix of debt and equity. This book uses:

```{math}
\text{NOPAT}=\text{Normalized EBIT}(1-T),
```

```{math}
\text{Invested capital}
=\text{Operating assets}
-\text{Non-interest-bearing operating liabilities}.
```

Under a consistently defined perimeter, the financing-side bridge is:

```{math}
\text{Invested capital}
=\text{Interest-bearing debt}
+\text{Equity claims}
-\text{Excess cash}
-\text{Other non-operating assets}.
```

The exact invested-capital bridge must state its treatment of leases, goodwill,
other investments, noncontrolling interests, and underfunded pensions.
ROIC definitions vary across analytical systems, so a valuation must preserve
one stated definition through history, forecast, and comparison
{cite}`damodaran2012investment`.

With EBIT of USD 150m and $T=25\%$:

```{math}
\text{NOPAT}=150(1-0.25)=112.5.
```

Using debt plus equity minus all cash as a simplified approximation:

```{math}
\text{Invested capital}_{t-1}=250+390-80=560,
```

```{math}
\text{Invested capital}_{t}=230+455-60=625.
```

Therefore:

```{math}
\overline{\text{Invested capital}}
=\frac{560+625}{2}
=592.5,
```

```{math}
\text{ROIC}
=\frac{112.5}{592.5}
=18.99\%.
```

ROIC should ultimately be compared with a risk-consistent required return, but
that comparison belongs in Module 5. Here the focus is measurement. If cash is
required for normal operations, subtracting all cash overstates ROIC; only
excess cash should be removed.

## Working-capital days

Average balances connect the statements to operating processes. When the
required operating data are available, use net credit sales for receivables and
credit purchases for payables:

```{math}
\text{DSO}
=\frac{\text{Average receivables}}{\text{Net credit sales}}\times365,
```

```{math}
\text{DIO}
=\frac{\text{Average inventory}}{\text{COGS}}\times365,
```

```{math}
\text{DPO}
=\frac{\text{Average payables}}{\text{Credit purchases}}\times365.
```

If all inventory purchases are recorded in the selected inventory and COGS
perimeter, purchases reconcile as:

```{math}
\text{Purchases}_t
=\mathrm{COGS}_t+\mathrm{Inventory}_t-\mathrm{Inventory}_{t-1}.
```

When credit-sales or credit-purchases data are unavailable, total revenue and
COGS can be disclosed as approximations:

```{math}
\mathrm{DSO}^{\mathrm{revenue\ proxy}}
=\frac{\text{Average receivables}}{\text{Revenue}}\times365,
```

```{math}
\mathrm{DPO}^{\mathrm{COGS\ proxy}}
=\frac{\text{Average payables}}{\mathrm{COGS}}\times365.
```

The cash conversion cycle (CCC) preserves the financing role of payables:

```{math}
\mathrm{CCC}=\mathrm{DSO}+\mathrm{DIO}-\mathrm{DPO}.
```

For the simulation, credit sales are unavailable and the table deliberately
uses the revenue- and COGS-based proxies:

| Measure | Calculation | Result |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| Revenue-based DSO proxy | $130/1{,}000\times365$ | 47.5 days |
| Days inventory outstanding (DIO) | $145/600\times365$ | 88.2 days |
| COGS-based DPO proxy | $102.5/600\times365$ | 62.4 days |
| Proxy cash conversion cycle | DSO proxy + DIO - DPO proxy | 73.3 days |

If the simulation additionally assumes that all USD 610m of purchases were on
credit while DSO remains revenue-based, the mixed-perimeter result changes:

```{math}
\begin{aligned}
\text{Purchases}_t&=600+(150-140)=610,\\
\mathrm{DPO}&=\frac{102.5}{610}\times365=61.3\text{ days},\\
\mathrm{CCC}^{\mathrm{mixed}}
&=47.5+88.2-61.3=74.4\text{ days}.
\end{aligned}
```

The displayed components round to one decimal, so their displayed sum is 74.4
days; using unrounded components gives 74.3 days. Do not mix an exact DPO with a
proxy DSO without labeling the resulting CCC as mixed-perimeter.

A longer cash conversion cycle or proxy can indicate weaker collections, excess
inventory, supplier terms, business growth, seasonality, or a changed sales
mix. The ratio identifies a question; the notes and operating data answer it.

## Liquidity, leverage, and coverage

At year-end, assume all receivables, inventory, and payables are current and no
debt matures within one year:

```{math}
\text{Current ratio}
=\frac{60+140+150}{105}
=3.33\times,
```

```{math}
\text{Quick ratio}
=\frac{60+140}{105}
=1.90\times.
```

With EBITDA of $150+50=200$:

```{math}
\text{Net debt/EBITDA}
=\frac{230-60}{200}
=0.85\times,
```

```{math}
\text{EBIT interest coverage}
=\frac{150}{30}
=5.00\times.
```

These calculations are intentionally simple. A real analysis must identify
restricted cash, short-term borrowings, leases, guarantees, securitizations,
hybrid securities, pension deficits, and the exact EBITDA adjustments used in
debt agreements.

## Interpretation discipline

Use three comparison axes:

1. **Time series:** the same issuer through at least one operating cycle.
2. **Cross section:** peers with comparable economics, accounting, and fiscal
   periods.
3. **Expectation:** budget, guidance, lender covenant, or valuation assumption.

Then trace each important change into a driver tree. For ROIC:

```{math}
\text{ROIC}
=
\underbrace{\frac{\text{NOPAT}}{\text{Revenue}}}_{\text{after-tax operating margin}}
\times
\underbrace{\frac{\text{Revenue}}{\text{Average invested capital}}}_{\text{capital turnover}}.
```

This makes the operating question explicit: did return change because of
pricing and cost control, or because of the capital required to support sales?

## Checks and limitations

- A ratio is not comparable when its accounting perimeter or definition
  changes.
- Period-end balances can distort highly seasonal businesses; prefer quarterly
  averages or daily data when material.
- EBITDA is not cash flow and excludes working capital, capital expenditures,
  taxes, and debt service.
- ROE can reward leverage, while ROIC can be distorted by goodwill,
  impairments, and the definition of excess cash.
- Industry economics matter. Bank and insurer ratios require specialized
  balance-sheet and regulatory measures developed in Lesson 4.4.
- A covenant ratio must follow the legal agreement, not an analyst's generic
  formula.

## Practice

An issuer reports revenue of USD 750m, COGS of USD 450m, EBIT of USD 90m,
depreciation of USD 30m, net income available to common shareholders of USD
45m, average assets of USD 600m, average common equity of USD 250m, average debt
of USD 220m, average cash of USD 40m, and interest expense of USD 18m.
Period-end debt and cash are USD 225m and USD 45m. Average receivables,
inventory, and payables are USD 75m, USD 90m, and USD 65m. Use $T=25\%$ and
365 days.

1. Decompose ROE using net margin, asset turnover, and equity multiplier.
2. Calculate ROIC using debt plus equity minus cash as invested capital.
3. Calculate net debt/EBITDA and EBIT interest coverage.
4. Calculate the revenue- and COGS-based proxy cash conversion cycle because
   credit sales and credit purchases are not provided.
5. Identify one limitation of each result.

```{dropdown} Suggested answer
Net margin is 6.0%, asset turnover is 1.25×, and the equity multiplier is 2.40×,
so ROE is $6.0\%\times1.25\times2.40=18.0\%$, equal to $45/250$.
NOPAT is USD 67.5m and approximate invested capital is
$220+250-40=430$, so ROIC is 15.70%. EBITDA is USD 120m; net debt/EBITDA is
based on period-end debt and cash and equals
$(225-45)/120=1.50\times$; EBIT interest coverage is 5.00×. The revenue-based
DSO proxy is 36.5 days, DIO is 73.0 days, the COGS-based DPO proxy is 52.7 days,
and the proxy cash conversion cycle is 56.8 days.

ROE is limited by the leverage embedded in the equity multiplier; ROIC uses an
approximate invested-capital and cash perimeter. Net debt/EBITDA and coverage
omit lease, maturity, covenant, and EBITDA-definition evidence. The working-
capital result uses revenue and COGS proxies rather than credit sales and credit
purchases, and annual averages may hide seasonality.
```
