# Analysis Framework and Statement Linkages

Module: Financial Statement Analysis and Financial Modeling

## Lesson summary

Financial statement analysis begins with a question and an evidence hierarchy.
An equity analyst, lender, supplier, and acquisition team may read the same
filing but emphasize different cash flows, claims, and risks. A reliable
analysis records that purpose, identifies the controlling documents, and
reconciles the statements before calculating ratios or making forecasts.

This lesson follows one simplified annual example from reported earnings to
cash and ending equity. The example is a simulation in USD millions and does
not represent a real issuer.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, readers should be able to:

- define the purpose, horizon, and decision context of an analysis;
- distinguish filings, notes, management commentary, and the auditor's report;
- explain the role of the income statement, balance sheet, cash flow statement,
  and statement of changes in equity;
- reconcile net income to operating cash flow and closing cash;
- verify the balance sheet identity and retained-earnings roll-forward;
- document comparability limitations before using ratios.

## Prerequisites and notation

Complete the [Module 4 overview](../../chapters/04-financial-statements-modeling.md).
The example uses:

- $R$: revenue during the year;
- $COGS$: cost of goods sold;
- $EBIT$: earnings before interest and taxes;
- $NI$: net income;
- $\Delta X=X_t-X_{t-1}$: period change in balance $X$;
- $\mathrm{RE}$: retained earnings; and
- CFO, CFI, and CFF: operating, investing, and financing cash flow.

All amounts are USD millions for one 365-day fiscal year. On income and cash
flow statements, parentheses or a leading minus sign indicate an expense or
cash outflow; in a balance-sheet change column, they indicate a decrease in the
balance. In equations that build a subtotal, expenses and outlays are positive
magnitudes that are subtracted.

## Start with the analytical question

A disciplined workflow has five stages:

1. **Frame:** identify the decision, user, horizon, materiality threshold, and
   required comparisons.
2. **Collect:** preserve the controlling filing and all documents needed to
   interpret it.
3. **Reconcile:** prove that statement totals, cash movement, debt, and equity
   roll forward.
4. **Analyze:** calculate ratios and adjustments from defined inputs.
5. **Forecast and communicate:** convert supported operating drivers into
   scenarios and state what could invalidate the conclusion.

Beginning with a ratio often reverses this order. For example, a declining
operating margin may reflect weaker pricing power, an acquisition, a
reclassification, a shorter reporting period, or a one-time charge. The filing
and notes determine which interpretation is defensible.

## Evidence hierarchy

### Primary financial statements

The income statement reports performance over a period. The balance sheet
reports resources and claims at a date. The cash flow statement explains the
change in cash over a period. The statement of changes in equity explains
retained earnings, dividends, share issuance, repurchases, accumulated
comprehensive income, and other direct equity movements
{cite}`ifrsConceptualFramework2018,ias1FinancialStatements2026,ias7CashFlows2026`.

### Notes

Notes are part of the financial statements, not optional background. They
usually contain accounting policies, disaggregated balances, maturity tables,
commitments, contingencies, segment information, tax reconciliations, pension
assumptions, lease obligations, acquisitions, and related-party information.
A line item should not be forecast until its relevant note has been read.

### Management commentary

Management discussion can explain pricing, volume, mix, capacity, strategy,
capital allocation, and known uncertainties. It is useful evidence, but it is
also selective and forward-looking. Separate management's explanation from the
analyst's independent reconciliation.

### Auditor's report

The auditor's report identifies the statements covered, the framework applied,
and the opinion. An unmodified opinion is not a guarantee of future viability
or the economic accuracy of every estimate. Record separately (i) modified
opinions: qualified, adverse, or a disclaimer of opinion; (ii) audit conditions
that may lead to a modification, such as an inability to obtain sufficient
evidence because of a scope limitation; (iii) a material uncertainty related
to going concern; and (iv) an emphasis-of-matter paragraph. These categories
are not interchangeable. An emphasis-of-matter paragraph does not modify the
opinion, and a scope limitation may lead to a qualified opinion or disclaimer
depending on materiality and pervasiveness. Trace each relevant matter to the
affected accounts and use the terminology of the applicable assurance standard
{cite}`iaasbISA700Revised2015,iaasbISA705Revised2015,iaasbISA706Revised2015`.
For going-concern reporting, ISA 570 (Revised 2015) remains the applicable
international baseline before the effective date of ISA 570 (Revised 2024).
The 2024 revision applies to audits of financial statements for periods
beginning on or after 15 December 2026, so the analysis must record the
reporting period and the edition actually applied
{cite}`iaasbISA570Revised2015,iaasbISA570Revised2024`.

## The three statements and equity bridge

### Income statement

For the simulated company:

| Income statement | USD m |
| --- | ---: |
| Revenue | 1,000 |
| Cost of goods sold | (600) |
| Operating expenses excluding depreciation | (200) |
| Depreciation | (50) |
| EBIT | 150 |
| Interest expense | (30) |
| Income tax expense | (30) |
| Net income | 90 |

The subtotals reconcile:

```{math}
\text{EBIT}=1{,}000-600-200-50=150,
```

```{math}
\text{Net income}=150-30-30=90.
```

Revenue and expenses describe accounting performance, not necessarily current
cash receipts and payments. The balance sheet changes reveal the timing
differences.

### Operating cash flow

Assume accounts receivable rose by USD 20m, inventory by USD 10m, and accounts
payable by USD 5m. Also assume depreciation is the only non-cash income
statement item and tax expense equals cash taxes for this example:

```{math}
\mathrm{CFO}_t
=\mathrm{NI}_t
+\text{Non-cash charges}_t
-\Delta\text{Operating current assets}_t
+\Delta\text{Operating current liabilities}_t.
```

Applying that indirect-method bridge to the selected accounts gives:

```{math}
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{CFO}_t
&=\mathrm{NI}_t+\text{Depreciation}_t
-\Delta\text{Receivables}
-\Delta\text{Inventory}
+\Delta\text{Payables}\\
&=90+50-20-10+5\\
&=115.
\end{aligned}
```

The signs express cash logic:

- higher receivables mean recognized revenue not yet collected;
- higher inventory means cash invested before sale;
- higher payables mean supplier financing;
- depreciation reduced earnings without a current-period cash payment.

### Investing and financing cash flow

Assume capital expenditures of USD 90m, debt repayment of USD 20m, and
dividends paid of USD 25m. The simulation assumes that dividends declared equal
dividends paid, there is no dividend payable, the cash scope is identical
between statements, and the exchange-rate effect on cash is zero:

| Cash flow section | USD m |
| --- | ---: |
| CFO | 115 |
| CFI: capital expenditures | (90) |
| CFF: debt repayment | (20) |
| CFF: dividends | (25) |
| Change in cash | (20) |

Therefore:

```{math}
\begin{aligned}
\Delta\text{Cash}_t
&=\mathrm{CFO}_t+\mathrm{CFI}_t+\mathrm{CFF}_t\\
&=115-90-20-25\\
&=-20.
\end{aligned}
```

Beginning cash of USD 80m becomes ending cash of USD 60m.

### Balance sheet and retained earnings

| Balance sheet | Beginning | Ending | Change |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Cash | 80 | 60 | (20) |
| Accounts receivable | 120 | 140 | 20 |
| Inventory | 140 | 150 | 10 |
| Net property, plant, and equipment | 400 | 440 | 40 |
| **Total assets** | **740** | **790** | **50** |
| Accounts payable | 100 | 105 | 5 |
| Debt | 250 | 230 | (20) |
| Equity | 390 | 455 | 65 |
| **Total liabilities and equity** | **740** | **790** | **50** |

Net property, plant, and equipment rolls forward as:

```{math}
\begin{aligned}
\text{Net PP\&E}_t
&=\text{Net PP\&E}_{t-1}
+\text{Capital expenditures}_t
-\text{Depreciation}_t\\
&=400+90-50\\
&=440.
\end{aligned}
```

With no other equity movements, retained earnings and total equity roll forward
together:

```{math}
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{RE}_t
&=\mathrm{RE}_{t-1}+\mathrm{NI}_t-\text{Dividends declared}_t\\
&=390+90-25\\
&=455.
\end{aligned}
```

Finally:

```{math}
\begin{aligned}
\text{Assets}_t
&=\text{Liabilities}_t+\text{Equity}_t\\
&=105+230+455\\
&=790.
\end{aligned}
```

The four reconciliations—cash, fixed assets, retained earnings, and the balance
sheet—are more informative than a statement that the model "looks reasonable."

## Comparability before ratios

Record at least these dimensions before comparing two periods or companies:

| Dimension | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Scope | Consolidated group, parent-only entity, or segment? |
| Period | Same number of weeks or months and same fiscal season? |
| Currency and unit | Same currency and scale, with translation policy known? |
| Accounting policy | Same recognition, cost-flow, lease, and depreciation choices? |
| Corporate perimeter | Any acquisition, disposal, discontinued operation, or new joint venture? |
| Inflation and FX | Nominal growth, constant-currency growth, or real growth? |
| Restatement | Are both observations based on the same filing vintage? |
| Audit status | Audited annual, reviewed interim, or management-only value? |

A percentage change is not interpretable until its numerator, denominator,
period, and corporate perimeter are comparable.

## Checks and limitations

The worked example intentionally excludes leases, deferred taxes, stock-based
compensation, foreign-currency translation, other comprehensive income,
acquisitions, asset disposals, and restricted cash. Real statements require
additional schedules.

Common failure modes include:

- using a press-release subtotal when the filing defines it differently;
- mixing year-to-date income with point-in-time balance sheet changes from the
  wrong dates;
- treating cash equivalents, restricted cash, and marketable securities as one
  balance without support;
- forgetting non-cash investing or financing activities;
- assuming the cash flow statement's classification policy is identical across
  issuers;
- accepting a balance-sheet plug instead of resolving the missing schedule.

## Practice

A company reports net income of USD 64m, depreciation of USD 18m, an increase
in receivables of USD 9m, a decrease in inventory of USD 4m, and a decrease in
payables of USD 3m. Capital expenditures are USD 35m, new debt issuance is USD
10m, dividends are USD 12m, and beginning cash is USD 40m.

1. Calculate CFO.
2. Calculate the change in cash.
3. Calculate ending cash.
4. State why a decrease in payables is a cash outflow.

```{dropdown} Suggested answer
CFO is $64+18-9+4-3=74$ USD m. CFI is $-35$ USD m and CFF is
$10-12=-2$ USD m, so cash increases by $74-35-2=37$ USD m. Ending cash is
USD 77m. A decrease in payables means the company paid suppliers for more than
the current period's newly deferred purchases, so cash use exceeds the expense
recognized through that working-capital account.
```
