# Economic Foundations for Investors

Module: Economics, Macro, and Currency

## Lesson summary

Investment analysis starts with prices, but prices are not isolated numbers. They are produced by demand, supply, constraints, market power, policy, expectations, and adjustment costs. This lesson gives the economic vocabulary needed before reading macro releases or currency moves {cite}`mishkin2019financial`.

The goal is not to turn the book into a full microeconomics text. The goal is to keep a compact decision framework that helps an analyst interpret market observations.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, readers should be able to:

- distinguish movement along a curve from a shift in demand or supply;
- interpret elasticity as sensitivity, not as a fixed universal number;
- explain surpluses, shortages, and price controls;
- compare perfect competition, monopoly, oligopoly, and differentiated markets;
- connect market structure with pricing power, margins, pass-through, and investment risk.

## Prerequisites and notation

Students should understand percentages, revenue as price times quantity, and
the difference between an observation and a causal claim from
[Module 1](../../chapters/01-markets-data.md). Let $P$ denote price, $Q$
quantity, revenue $PQ$, and unit variable cost $c$. Contribution margin is
$(P-c)Q$.

## Supply and demand discipline

When a price changes, ask whether the event changed quantity demanded, demand,
quantity supplied, or supply. Price and quantity alone do not identify the
shock: the candidate readings below require the other curve to remain stable,
or one shift to dominate simultaneous changes.

| Observation | Candidate interpretation under a stable counterpart curve | Investment implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Price rises and quantity rises | Demand likely shifted outward. | Stronger demand may support revenue, margins, or inflation pressure. |
| Price rises and quantity falls | Supply likely shifted inward. | Cost shocks can lift prices while hurting volume and margins. |
| Price falls and quantity rises | Supply likely shifted outward. | Productivity or capacity gains can pressure prices but expand volume. |
| Price falls and quantity falls | Demand likely shifted inward. | Weak demand can pressure sales, credit quality, and risk appetite. |

```{figure} ../../img/generated/m3-supply-demand-identification.png
:alt: Two schematic supply-and-demand panels. An inward supply shift raises price and lowers quantity along one demand curve, while an outward demand shift raises both price and quantity along one supply curve.
:width: 900px
:name: module-3-supply-demand-identification

The same price direction can come from different shocks. Identifying the
mechanism requires a stable counterpart curve or additional evidence; the
schematic curves are not estimated data.
```

Elasticity summarizes sensitivity. For two observed points, the midpoint or
arc convention is:

```{math}
\varepsilon^{\mathrm{arc}}_{Q,P}
=
\frac{(Q_2-Q_1)/[(Q_1+Q_2)/2]}
{(P_2-P_1)/[(P_1+P_2)/2]}.
```

In financial interpretation, elasticity is most useful as a question: how much can volume, margin, import demand, loan demand, or currency demand change when prices, rates, or exchange rates move?

## Worked example: observed arc elasticity and revenue

Suppose a producer raises price from MXN 100 to MXN 110 and observed quantity
falls from 1,000 to 920 units. Using the midpoint convention,

```{math}
\%\Delta Q=\frac{920-1{,}000}{(920+1{,}000)/2}=-8.333\%,
```

```{math}
\%\Delta P=\frac{110-100}{(110+100)/2}=9.524\%,
```

so the observed midpoint arc elasticity over this interval is

```{math}
\varepsilon^{\mathrm{arc}}_{Q,P}=\frac{-8.333\%}{9.524\%}=-0.875.
```

Revenue rises from MXN 100,000 to MXN 101,200, an increase of 1.2%. The
absolute elasticity is below one, so the revenue direction is consistent with
movement along a stable, inelastic demand curve. It does not identify a demand
curve or prove permanent pricing power: competitor response, product mix,
seasonality, and simultaneous demand or supply shifts could also explain the
two observations.

For a differentiable demand curve and a local price change, the revenue link is
especially compact:

```{math}
R(P)=P Q(P),
\qquad
\frac{\mathrm{d}\ln R}{\mathrm{d}\ln P}
=1+\varepsilon_{Q,P}.
```

Thus a point elasticity above $-1$ implies that a small price increase raises
revenue along that stable curve. The midpoint elasticity above is a finite-
interval estimate, so this differential relation supports the direction but
does not make the two-point estimate a structural demand model.

### Revenue is not contribution margin

Suppose unit variable cost rises from MXN 70 to MXN 78 over the same interval.
Contribution margin changes from

```{math}
\mathrm{CM}_1=(100-70)(1{,}000)=\text{MXN }30{,}000
```

to

```{math}
\mathrm{CM}_2=(110-78)(920)=\text{MXN }29{,}440,
\qquad
\frac{\mathrm{CM}_2}{\mathrm{CM}_1}-1=-1.867\%.
```

Revenue rises while contribution margin falls. An investment conclusion
therefore needs cost pass-through and unit-cost evidence in addition to price,
quantity, and elasticity.

## Price controls and adjustment frictions

Price controls, subsidies, taxes, tariffs, capital controls, and regulated rates can separate the observed price from the clearing price. That gap matters because it may create shortages, surpluses, fiscal costs, hidden liabilities, or sudden repricing risk when the control changes.

For investment notes, document:

- the controlled price or regulated variable;
- the market-clearing pressure it may be hiding;
- who benefits and who absorbs the cost;
- whether the policy is fiscally sustainable;
- what market data would reveal stress before adjustment.

## Market structure

Market structure affects pricing power and pass-through.

| Structure | Key feature | Market signal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Perfect competition | Many firms, weak pricing power | Margins adjust quickly to costs and demand. |
| Monopoly | One seller protected by high barriers to entry | Regulation, substitution, and political risk matter. |
| Oligopoly | Few strategic competitors | Pricing can be sticky, coordinated, or unstable. |
| Monopolistic competition | Differentiated products compete on brand, quality, network, or location | Margins depend on product advantage and customer switching costs. |

In equity analysis, market structure affects revenue growth, margin durability, and valuation. In macro analysis, it affects inflation pass-through, wage bargaining, and policy transmission.

## Analyst checklist

Use this compact checklist before moving into macro indicators:

1. What price changed?
2. Did demand, supply, or both shift?
3. Is the market competitive, concentrated, regulated, or constrained?
4. Are consumers or firms likely to absorb the shock?
5. Does the change affect expected cash flows, discount rates, risk premia, or currency conversion?
