# Currency, Parity, and FX

Module: Economics, Macro, and Currency

## Lesson summary

Currency analysis links macro fundamentals with market prices. Exchange rates affect imported inflation, export competitiveness, foreign-currency debt, international portfolio returns, hedging costs, and cross-border capital flows {cite}`mishkin2019financial`.

This lesson focuses on quote discipline and parity relationships. It is a structure for future FX notebooks, not a claim that parity conditions hold perfectly in every market.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, readers should be able to:

- define spot rates, cross-rates, direct quotes, and indirect quotes;
- avoid base-currency and quote-currency reversals;
- calculate triangular cross-rates;
- interpret forward premium and discount;
- state purchasing power parity, covered interest parity, and uncovered interest parity;
- explain why carry trades can earn returns and still carry crash risk;
- connect currency stress with balance-of-payments, policy credibility, and global risk appetite.

## Prerequisites and notation

Complete [Macro Indicators and Policy](3.2.macro_indicators_policy.md) first.
Students should be able to convert percent rates to decimals, compound a
one-period rate, and keep units attached to every ratio. Throughout this lesson,
Mexico is the domestic market, the United States is foreign, and rates are
effective one-year rates unless another horizon is stated.

| Symbol | Unit | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $S_t$ | MXN per USD | Spot quote at time $t$; an increase is USD appreciation and MXN depreciation. |
| $F_{t,T}$ | MXN per USD | Forward quote agreed at $t$ for settlement at $T$. |
| $i_{\mathrm{MXN}}$ | Decimal over one year | One-year effective rate on the matched MXN instrument. |
| $i_{\mathrm{USD}}$ | Decimal over one year | One-year effective rate on the matched USD instrument. |
| $R_{\mathrm{USD}}$ | Decimal over the horizon | USD return after converting into MXN and back at the future spot. |

## Quote convention

Let $S_{\text{MXN/USD}}$ be the number of Mexican pesos per one US dollar. If
$S_{\text{MXN/USD}}$ rises, the US dollar appreciates against the peso and the
peso depreciates against the US dollar.

From the perspective of a Mexican resident, MXN per USD is a **direct quote**:
units of domestic currency per unit of foreign currency. USD per MXN is the
**indirect quote**. Market tickers often write the base currency first—`USD/MXN`
for this same economic quote—so every table must state the dimensional unit
`MXN per USD` rather than rely on ticker typography alone.

The inverse quote is:

```{math}
S_{\text{USD/MXN}} = \frac{1}{S_{\text{MXN/USD}}}.
```

Percentage changes in reciprocal quotes have opposite signs but not identical
magnitudes. If $g_S=S_T/S_t-1$ is the change in MXN per USD, then

```{math}
g_{1/S}
=\frac{1/S_T}{1/S_t}-1
=\frac{S_t}{S_T}-1
=-\frac{g_S}{1+g_S}.
```

Here $g_{1/S}$ is the actual change in USD per MXN. This asymmetry is why a
10% rise in one quote is not a 10% fall in its reciprocal.

Every FX table should state the quote convention before any return, hedge, or parity calculation.

A unit check prevents most cross-rate reversals:

```{math}
\frac{\mathrm{MXN}}{\mathrm{USD}}
\times
\frac{\mathrm{USD}}{\mathrm{EUR}}
=
\frac{\mathrm{MXN}}{\mathrm{EUR}}.
```

## Worked example: one quote convention through cross-rates and parity

Let spot be $S_{\mathrm{MXN/USD}}=17.20$ and
$S_{\mathrm{USD/EUR}}=1.08$. The cross-rate is:

```{math}
S_{\mathrm{MXN/EUR}}=17.20\times1.08=18.576.
```

This means MXN 18.576 per EUR, not the inverse.

For a one-year horizon, let the MXN rate be 9.00% and the USD rate 5.00%.
Assume matched effective-rate instruments, unrestricted borrowing and lending,
common settlement, no transaction costs, and no cross-currency basis. Covered
interest parity then implies:

```{math}
F_{\mathrm{MXN/USD}}
=17.20\frac{1.09}{1.05}
=17.8552.
```

The USD is at a 3.81% forward premium in MXN terms; equivalently, the MXN is at
a forward discount under this quote. If expected Mexican and US inflation are
4.50% and 2.50%, relative PPP instead gives:

```{math}
S^{\mathrm{PPP}}_{t+1}
=17.20\frac{1.045}{1.025}
=17.5356.
```

These are different objects: CIP is an arbitrage relationship using tradable
interest rates and a forward; PPP is a goods-price benchmark and can deviate
substantially over a one-year horizon.

## Cross-rates and triangular arbitrage

If quotes are expressed as local currency per unit of foreign currency, a cross-rate can be built from two related rates:

```{math}
S_{\text{MXN/EUR}}
= S_{\text{MXN/USD}} \times S_{\text{USD/EUR}}.
```

Triangular arbitrage checks whether quoted cross-rates are internally consistent after transaction costs. In real markets, tiny deviations may persist because of bid-ask spreads, liquidity, settlement, credit, and execution frictions.

Suppose the directly quoted MXN/EUR rate were 18.80 while the synthetic rate
remained 18.576. Ignoring costs, borrow EUR 1, sell it for MXN 18.80, convert
the pesos into $18.80/17.20=1.0930$ USD, and buy
$1.0930/1.08=1.0121$ EUR. Repaying EUR 1 leaves about EUR 0.0121. A real
arbitrage test must use executable bid and ask quotes on every leg; midpoint
quotes cannot establish a tradable profit.

## Parity conditions

### Purchasing power parity

Purchasing power parity links exchange-rate changes to relative inflation:

```{math}
\frac{S_{t+k}}{S_t}
\approx
\frac{1+\pi^{\text{domestic}}_{t,t+k}}
{1+\pi^{\text{foreign}}_{t,t+k}}.
```

PPP is more useful as a long-horizon valuation anchor than as a short-horizon trading rule.

### Covered interest parity

Covered interest parity links spot FX, forward FX, and interest-rate
differentials when currency risk is hedged. With matched effective rates:

```{math}
\frac{F_{t,T}}{S_t}
=
\frac{1+i^{\text{domestic}}_{t,T}}
{1+i^{\text{foreign}}_{t,T}}.
```

Large deviations may indicate funding stress, capital controls, counterparty risk, collateral constraints, or data issues.

### Uncovered interest parity

Uncovered interest parity replaces the forward contract with an expected future spot rate:

```{math}
\mathbb{E}_t\left[\frac{S_T}{S_t}\right]
\approx
\frac{1+i^{\text{domestic}}_{t,T}}
{1+i^{\text{foreign}}_{t,T}}.
```

UIP is a benchmark for thinking about risk compensation, not a guarantee that high-yield currencies will depreciate exactly enough to erase carry returns.

## Carry return and currency stress

A carry trade borrows in a lower-yielding currency and invests in a higher-yielding currency. The return depends on the interest differential and the exchange-rate move. Carry can look attractive during stable risk conditions and unwind sharply when volatility, funding stress, or policy credibility concerns rise.

For a USD investor who converts into MXN at $S_t$, invests at the MXN rate,
and converts back at $S_T$, the unhedged USD return is:

```{math}
R_{\mathrm{USD}}
=(1+i_{\mathrm{MXN}})\frac{S_t}{S_T}-1.
```

Using the worked example's $S_t=17.20$, $i_{\mathrm{MXN}}=9.00\%$, and
$i_{\mathrm{USD}}=5.00\%$, an unchanged spot would produce 9.00% in USD
before costs, versus 5.00% on the USD instrument. The excess carry is erased at

```{math}
S_T^*
=S_t\frac{1+i_{\mathrm{MXN}}}{1+i_{\mathrm{USD}}}
=17.20\frac{1.09}{1.05}
=17.8552\ \frac{\mathrm{MXN}}{\mathrm{USD}}.
```

The break-even quote equals the CIP forward under the matched-instrument
assumptions. It is 3.810% above spot, so the peso depreciates by 3.810% in the
MXN-per-USD quote while the reciprocal USD-per-MXN quote falls by 3.670%. If
$S_T=18.00$, the unhedged MXN investment returns

```{math}
(1.09)\frac{17.20}{18.00}-1=4.156\%
```

in USD, underperforming the 5.00% USD alternative by 0.844 percentage points
before costs.

Peso depreciation lowers the USD value of the MXN proceeds. The intersection

Balance-of-payments accounts balance by construction; a current-account deficit
is not itself a crisis. Stress arises when the required financing cannot be
rolled over on acceptable terms. Adjustment can appear through reserve losses,
higher domestic rates, FX depreciation, weaker demand, or capital controls.
Assess the funding mix, maturity, foreign-currency debt, reserve adequacy,
policy credibility, and contingent liabilities before making a crisis claim.

Before making a currency claim, document:

- spot quote and direction;
- local and foreign interest rates;
- inflation differential;
- forward premium or discount;
- external balance and reserve context;
- foreign-currency debt exposure;
- central bank credibility;
- liquidity and transaction costs.
