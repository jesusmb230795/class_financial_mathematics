# Macro Indicators and Policy

Module: Economics, Macro, and Currency

## Lesson summary

Macro indicators are investment inputs when they change expectations about growth, inflation, rates, credit conditions, earnings, fiscal sustainability, or risk appetite. This lesson defines the minimum indicator map needed before building macro dashboards or capital market expectations {cite}`mishkin2019financial,banxicoCentralBank`.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, readers should be able to:

- distinguish level, growth rate, gap, and surprise for macro indicators;
- connect GDP, inflation, employment, productivity, and financial conditions to asset prices;
- explain the channels of monetary-policy transmission;
- explain fiscal deficits, public debt, sustainability, and crowding-out risk;
- read yield curves as both market prices and macro signals;
- document source, unit, frequency, revision, and lag before using a macro series.

## Prerequisites and notation

Complete [Economic Foundations for Investors](3.1.economic_foundations.md)
first. Students should be able to calculate percentage changes and one-period
present value and to distinguish a price change from a shift in an underlying
economic curve. Rates use decimal values in formulas. For fiscal arithmetic,
\(d_t\) denotes debt as a share of GDP and \(pb_t\) the primary surplus as a
share of GDP, so a deficit makes \(pb_t<0\).

## Indicator map

| Indicator family | Examples | Main investment question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Output and growth | GDP, industrial production, retail sales, output gap | Is activity expanding, slowing, or below potential? |
| Inflation | CPI, core CPI, producer prices, inflation expectations | Are real returns, margins, policy rates, and purchasing power under pressure? |
| Labor market | employment, unemployment, wages, participation | Is demand strong enough to support income, consumption, and wage pressure? |
| Productivity and demographics | productivity growth, labor force growth, dependency ratios | What is the long-run growth constraint? |
| Monetary policy | policy rate, liquidity, money supply, central bank guidance | Is policy restrictive, neutral, or accommodative? |
| Fiscal policy | deficit, debt, primary balance, tax revenue, spending | Is the public-sector path sustainable and how does it affect rates? |
| Financial conditions | yield curves, credit spreads, lending standards, FX pressure | Are financing conditions tightening or easing? |

For Mexico-focused work, Banxico and INEGI are preferred when they cover the required series. For cross-country or global comparisons, DB.NOMICS, World Bank, IMF WEO, and OECD data provide reproducible public source paths {cite}`banxicoSIE2025,inegiAPI2025,dbnomics2025,worldBankOpenData2025,imfWEO2025,oecdData2025`.

### Level, growth, gap, and surprise are different objects

| Object | Definition | Unit example | Investment use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Level | The observed value \(x_t\). | CPI index points or policy rate (%) | Describes the state, but not its pace or expectation error. |
| Growth | Change over a declared interval, such as \(x_t/x_{t-12}-1\). | Year-over-year inflation (%) | Measures direction and speed; base effects matter. |
| Gap | Difference from a named reference; displayed output gap: \(100(Y_t-Y_t^*)/Y_t^*\). | Percentage points from a rate target or output gap (% of potential GDP) | Measures slack or pressure, conditional on an estimated benchmark. |
| Surprise | Actual release minus the pre-release consensus. | Percentage points or standardized units | Isolates expectation error; record the consensus source and timestamp. |

Do not compare a level with a growth rate or treat an estimated gap as directly
observed. A surprise can move markets even when the released level is unchanged
because the prior expectation changed.

## Monetary policy channel

Banco de México states price stability as its main objective, and the target
for the overnight interbank interest rate is the operational reference for
monetary policy implementation {cite}`banxicoCentralBank,banxicoOperatingTarget`.

For effective nominal and real rates over the same horizon and compounding
convention, the exact Fisher relation is:

```{math}
1+\rho_t=\frac{1+i_t}{1+\pi^e_t}.
```

Its small-rate approximation is:

```{math}
\rho_t \approx i_t - \pi^e_t,
```

where \(\rho_t\) is the horizon-matched effective real rate, \(i_t\) is the
horizon-matched effective nominal market rate, and \(\pi^e_t\) is expected
inflation over that horizon. A quoted overnight policy
target cannot be inserted into a one-year Fisher calculation without an
explicit conversion or policy-path assumption. A higher real rate can reduce
demand, raise discount rates, support currency demand, and pressure
long-duration assets. A lower real rate can support borrowing and risk taking,
but may weaken the currency if inflation or credibility concerns rise.

### Worked example: real rate and valuation sensitivity

If the one-year nominal rate is 10.50% and expected inflation is 4.20%, the
approximate real rate is 6.30%. The exact Fisher calculation is:

```{math}
\rho_{\mathrm{exact}}
=\frac{1+0.105}{1+0.042}-1
=6.046\%.
```

Separately, hold a one-year expected cash flow at MXN 105. Discounting at 9.00% gives
MXN 96.33. If the relevant discount rate rises 150 basis points to 10.50%,
present value falls to MXN 95.02, a decline of about 1.36%. This isolates the
discount-rate channel; a full macro scenario must also allow the cash flow and
risk premium to change.

## Fiscal policy channel

Fiscal policy affects aggregate demand, public debt, sovereign risk, and the supply of government securities. The basic sustainability question is whether the primary balance and growth-rate path can stabilize debt without requiring inflation, default, restructuring, or unusually high future taxes.

For investment interpretation, separate:

- cyclical deficits caused by temporary weakness;
- structural deficits that persist even when the economy is near potential;
- debt held in local currency versus foreign currency;
- average maturity, rollover risk, and interest-rate sensitivity;
- fiscal credibility and the quality of spending.

### Worked example: debt-ratio arithmetic

Ignoring valuation effects and other stock-flow adjustments, a compact debt
identity is:

```{math}
d_t
=\frac{1+i_t}{1+g_t}d_{t-1}-pb_t,
```

where \(i_t\) is the effective nominal interest rate on the debt stock and
\(g_t\) nominal GDP growth over the same horizon. If prior debt is 50% of GDP,
\(i_t=8\%\), \(g_t=6\%\), and the primary surplus is 0.5% of GDP, then

```{math}
d_t=\frac{1.08}{1.06}(0.50)-0.005=0.5044.
```

Debt therefore rises by about 0.44 percentage points of GDP despite the
surplus. This is accounting, not a sustainability verdict: maturity, currency,
contingent liabilities, market access, and future policy responses also matter.

## Yield curves and financial conditions

Yield curves summarize market pricing of future rates, term premia, inflation expectations, liquidity, and credit conditions. A steep curve, flat curve, or inverted curve can carry different information depending on the policy regime and inflation context.

Do not read a curve shape mechanically. Record:

1. the curve source and instruments;
2. whether yields are nominal or real;
3. the relevant policy-rate path;
4. inflation expectations;
5. liquidity and credit spread context;
6. the asset class being analyzed.

For example, if homogeneous two-year and ten-year government yields are 9.40%
and 8.80%, the 10y-minus-2y slope is \(-60\) basis points. The curve is inverted
under that definition. This does not by itself prove a recession forecast:
expected policy easing, term premia, liquidity, inflation risk, and instrument
differences must still be assessed. A mixture of policy, CETES, TIIE, and
Treasury rates is a **rate panel**, not one yield curve.

## Checkpoint exercise

Assume a one-year nominal rate of 8.75%, expected inflation of 4.00%, and a
one-year expected cash flow of MXN 112.

1. Calculate the approximate and exact real rates.
2. Calculate present value at 8.75% and after a 100-basis-point rate increase,
   holding cash flow fixed.
3. Explain why the calculation is a partial-equilibrium sensitivity rather than
   a complete monetary-policy forecast.
4. With prior debt at 60% of GDP, an effective nominal interest rate of 9%,
   nominal GDP growth of 6%, and a primary deficit of 1% of GDP, calculate the
   next debt ratio under the simplified identity.
5. If homogeneous two-year and ten-year yields are 9.10% and 8.70%, report the
   10y-minus-2y slope and one limitation of a mechanical curve signal.

```{dropdown} Suggested answer
The approximate real rate is 4.75%; the exact rate is
\(1.0875/1.04-1=4.567\%\). Present value is MXN 102.99 at 8.75% and MXN 102.05
at 9.75%, a decline of approximately 0.91%. The sensitivity holds cash flow and
risk premium fixed even though a policy shock may change both, so it isolates
only one transmission channel. With a primary deficit, \(pb=-0.01\), the debt
ratio is \((1.09/1.06)(0.60)-(-0.01)=62.70\%\), an increase of 2.70 percentage
points of GDP. The 10y-minus-2y slope is -40 basis points. It is inverted under
that definition, but term premia, liquidity, instrument comparability, and the
policy regime limit a mechanical macro interpretation.
```
