# Business Model and Forecasting

Module: Corporate Issuers and Equity Valuation

## Lesson summary

A forecast is a quantified explanation of how a business competes. Revenue
growth without customers, volume, price, retention, capacity, or market share
is only an extrapolation. Margin forecasts without supplier power, product mix,
cost structure, and reinvestment are equally incomplete.

This lesson links business-model analysis, Porter's Five Forces, PESTLE,
pricing power, and unit economics to a driver-based forecast. The worked
example is a simulated subscription business in USD millions.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, readers should be able to:

- map how a company acquires customers, delivers value, earns revenue, incurs
  costs, and reinvests;
- translate Porter's Five Forces and PESTLE factors into forecast variables;
- distinguish price increases from pricing power;
- calculate customer, revenue, contribution, EBIT, and FCFF drivers;
- design internally coherent downside, base, and upside cases;
- identify circular reasoning, market-share impossibilities, and terminal
  assumptions that exceed competitive evidence.

## Prerequisites and notation

Complete [Capital Structure and WACC](5.2.capital_structure_and_wacc.md) and the
Module 4 three-statement model.

- \(C_0\), \(C_1\): beginning and ending customers;
- \(A\): additions during the period;
- \(Ch\): customer churn during the period;
- \(ARPU\): annual revenue per average customer;
- \(GM\): gross margin;
- \(T\): normalized cash tax rate.

All rates are decimals in calculations. The example is annual and uses average
customers \((C_0+C_1)/2\).

## Business-model map

Document six connected components:

| Component | Evidence | Forecast output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Customer and need | Segment, use case, switching cost, willingness to pay | Addressable demand, acquisition, retention |
| Product and delivery | Features, quality, capacity, channel, service obligation | Volume, utilization, service cost, capital expenditure |
| Revenue model | Unit price, subscription, transaction fee, spread, license, advertising | Price, volume, mix, timing, contract liability |
| Cost structure | Variable inputs, labor, distribution, technology, fixed overhead | Gross margin, contribution, operating leverage |
| Capital model | Inventory, receivables, fixed assets, intangibles, regulation | Working capital, reinvestment, ROIC, FCFF |
| Competitive advantage | Scale, network, brand, cost, data, contracts, regulation | Retention, pricing, margins, fade period |

The map should reconcile to reported segments and statement lines. A strategic
claim that cannot be connected to a measurable operating or cash-flow driver
does not yet support valuation.

## Industry analysis

### Porter's Five Forces

Translate each force into a testable driver:

| Force | Analytical question | Possible forecast effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rivalry | How many credible alternatives compete on price, quality, or capacity? | Price, churn, marketing, margin |
| Threat of entry | What capital, distribution, regulation, data, or trust barriers exist? | Market share, reinvestment, terminal fade |
| Supplier power | Are inputs concentrated, scarce, differentiated, or regulated? | COGS, working capital, capacity |
| Buyer power | Can customers switch, negotiate, integrate backward, or delay purchase? | Price, contract term, DSO, retention |
| Substitutes | Can a different product or behavior solve the same need? | Addressable market, price ceiling, obsolescence |

A framework score is not the conclusion. Cite contract evidence, market shares,
capacity, customer cohorts, procurement disclosures, and competitor actions.

### PESTLE

PESTLE tests external conditions:

- **Political:** industrial policy, trade relationships, public procurement;
- **Economic:** growth, rates, inflation, wages, FX, credit conditions;
- **Social:** demographics, trust, preferences, labor availability;
- **Technological:** automation, platforms, cybersecurity, substitution;
- **Legal:** licenses, competition, privacy, labor, contracts, tax;
- **Environmental:** physical risk, transition policy, energy, water, waste.

Each material factor needs a transmission chain. For example:

```text
MXN depreciation
→ imported input cost
→ gross margin or price response
→ customer retention and working capital
→ FCFF and value.
```

Avoid listing macro or regulatory facts without connecting them to a statement
line, timing, and scenario.

## Pricing power

A price increase is an action; pricing power is the ability to sustain a higher
price without losing so much volume, mix, retention, or reputation that
economic profit falls.

Measure:

- realized price versus list price;
- volume and churn response by cohort;
- discounts, rebates, promotions, and contract renewal;
- competitor price and capacity;
- unit contribution after service and acquisition cost;
- receivable days and bad debt after a price change.

Inflation pass-through that merely preserves real unit margin is different from
an increase in economic pricing power.

## Unit economics

Choose units that match the business:

- customer or cohort for subscriptions;
- order or gross merchandise value for platforms;
- unit, tonne, room, seat, or kilometer for physical capacity;
- loan, deposit, or policy for financial services.

For a subscription model:

```{math}
C_1=C_0-Ch+A,
```

```{math}
\text{Revenue}
=\frac{C_0+C_1}{2}\times ARPU,
```

```{math}
\text{Gross profit}=\text{Revenue}\times GM.
```

Customer acquisition cost, retention, gross contribution, support load, and
capital needs should be measured by cohort. A simple
\(1/\text{churn rate}\) lifetime estimate can be misleading when cohorts,
discounting, contraction, expansion, or churn timing vary.

## Worked example: base forecast

Assume:

- beginning customers: 100,000;
- annual churn: 8,000 customers;
- new customers: 30,000;
- annual ARPU: USD 240;
- gross margin: 70%;
- fixed cash operating expense: USD 10.0m;
- D&A: USD 2.0m;
- capital expenditure: USD 2.5m;
- increase in operating working capital: USD 0.6m;
- cash tax rate: 25%.

Ending and average customers are:

```{math}
C_1=100{,}000-8{,}000+30{,}000=122{,}000,
```

```{math}
\overline{C}=\frac{100{,}000+122{,}000}{2}=111{,}000.
```

Revenue is:

```{math}
111{,}000\times240
=26.640\text{ USD m}.
```

| Forecast line | USD m |
| --- | ---: |
| Revenue | 26.640 |
| Service and variable cost | (7.992) |
| Gross profit | 18.648 |
| Fixed cash operating expense | (10.000) |
| EBITDA | 8.648 |
| D&A | (2.000) |
| EBIT | 6.648 |
| NOPAT at 25% | 4.986 |

FCFF is:

```{math}
\text{FCFF}
=4.986+2.000-2.500-0.600
=3.886.
```

The forecast exposes its economics: customer retention and acquisition drive
the customer base; ARPU and average customers drive revenue; gross margin and
fixed cost drive operating leverage; capital expenditure and working capital
convert profit into cash.

## Scenario design

Use connected cases:

| Driver | Downside | Base | Upside |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Beginning customers | 100,000 | 100,000 | 100,000 |
| Customer churn | 12,000 | 8,000 | 6,000 |
| New customers | 20,000 | 30,000 | 35,000 |
| ARPU | USD 235 | USD 240 | USD 245 |
| Gross margin | 65% | 70% | 72% |
| Fixed cash operating expense | USD 10.0m | USD 10.0m | USD 10.5m |
| D&A | USD 2.0m | USD 2.0m | USD 2.2m |
| Capital expenditure | USD 2.5m | USD 2.5m | USD 3.0m |
| Increase in working capital | USD 0.6m | USD 0.6m | USD 0.7m |

The downside represents weaker retention and acquisition, lower realized
price, and service-cost pressure. The upside spends more to support growth
rather than assuming growth is free.

Before using these cases in valuation, extend them through the explicit
forecast and test:

- customer count against addressable market;
- acquisition against sales capacity and acquisition cost;
- gross margin against service quality and supplier power;
- fixed cost against required platform and control investment;
- working capital and capital expenditure against the operating plan;
- terminal growth and margin against competitive fade.

## Forecast review

A forecast review should answer:

1. Which three variables explain most of revenue change?
2. Which costs are variable, step-fixed, or truly fixed over the horizon?
3. What reinvestment is required before the forecasted revenue is earned?
4. Which competitor, customer, supplier, regulation, or technology fact would
   invalidate the base case?
5. Does the forecast imply market share, margins, or ROIC above the evidence?
6. How quickly do excess returns fade toward a sustainable competitive state?

Competitive analysis and valuation are inseparable because terminal value often
depends more on the duration of excess returns than on near-term growth
{cite}`damodaran2012investment`.

## Checks and limitations

- Total addressable market is not a revenue forecast.
- Customer additions without acquisition cost or capacity are incomplete.
- Average-customer formulas can hide monthly churn and seasonality.
- Price, volume, and mix effects should not be counted twice.
- Fixed cost can become step-fixed when capacity thresholds are crossed.
- A high gross margin can coexist with poor cash economics when acquisition,
  working capital, or capital expenditure is large.
- Scenario labels are conditional cases, not probabilities unless supported by
  an explicit probability model.

## Practice

A subscription company begins with 50,000 customers, loses 5,000, and adds
15,000 during the year. ARPU is USD 300, gross margin is 65%, fixed cash
operating expense is USD 6.0m, D&A is USD 1.0m, capital expenditure is USD
1.5m, and operating working capital increases by USD 0.3m. Use a 25% tax rate.

1. Calculate ending and average customers.
2. Calculate revenue, gross profit, EBIT, NOPAT, and FCFF.
3. Explain how a two-percentage-point increase in churn should flow through a
   full forecast.

```{dropdown} Suggested answer
Ending customers are 60,000 and average customers are 55,000. Revenue is USD
16.50m and gross profit is USD 10.725m. EBITDA is USD 4.725m, EBIT is USD
3.725m, and NOPAT is USD 2.794m. FCFF is
\(2.794+1.000-1.500-0.300=1.994\) USD m. Higher churn lowers ending and average
customers, revenue, and contribution; it may also require more acquisition
spending to reach the target customer count, change working capital and
capacity needs, reduce FCFF, and shorten the supported excess-return period.
```
