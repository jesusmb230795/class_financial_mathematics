# Corporate Decisions and Capital Budgeting

Module: Corporate Issuers and Equity Valuation

## Lesson summary

Capital allocation turns strategy into cash commitments. A company can invest
in working capital, capacity, technology, acquisitions, or financial assets;
reduce debt; or distribute cash through dividends and repurchases. Governance
determines who proposes, challenges, approves, monitors, and stops those uses
of capital.

This lesson uses incremental after-tax cash flow to compare NPV, IRR, payback,
profitability index, ROIC, and real-options reasoning. The numerical cases are
simulations in USD millions.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, readers should be able to:

- map governance rights and stakeholder constraints into a capital-allocation
  process;
- distinguish incremental cash flow from accounting earnings and sunk costs;
- calculate NPV, IRR, simple payback, and profitability index;
- explain why NPV is the primary value-additivity criterion;
- connect project ROIC with reinvestment and value creation;
- identify options to defer, expand, contract, stage, or abandon a project;
- compare reinvestment, liquidity, debt reduction, dividends, and repurchases.

## Prerequisites and notation

Complete [Module 4](../../chapters/04-financial-statements-modeling.md),
especially [Three-Statement Model and Review Memo](4.5.three_statement_model_and_review.md).

$I_0$ is the time-zero incremental investment, $CF_t$ is the after-tax
incremental cash flow at time $t$, $r$ is the project-specific required
return, and $N$ is the explicit horizon. Rates are decimals in formulas. Unless
stated otherwise, cash flows occur at year-end and $r$ is an effective annual
rate on the same nominal or real basis as the cash flows.

## Governance and stakeholders

Corporate decisions distribute risk and value across shareholders, employees,
customers, suppliers, lenders, governments, communities, and management.
Governance does not eliminate trade-offs; it establishes decision rights,
evidence standards, incentives, controls, and accountability
{cite}`oecd2023CorporateGovernance`.

A capital-allocation register should record:

| Field | Decision question |
| --- | --- |
| Sponsor and owner | Who proposes the project and who owns delivery? |
| Strategic rationale | Which customer, capability, risk, or constraint does it address? |
| Incremental cash flow | What changes only if the project proceeds? |
| Funding and liquidity | When is cash committed and what contingency is available? |
| Risk and hurdle rate | Which risks are diversifiable, systematic, contractual, or operational? |
| Approval rights | Which board, committee, lender, regulator, or partner must consent? |
| Stage gates | Which evidence permits further spending? |
| Post-audit | When will actual volume, price, cost, timing, and cash be compared with approval? |

Agency conflicts arise when decision makers receive upside but do not bear the
same downside as capital providers or other stakeholders. Independent review,
scenario analysis, compensation design, disclosure, and post-investment audits
help align decisions, but they do not substitute for sound cash-flow estimates
{cite}`jensenMeckling1976TheoryFirm`.

## Working capital and liquidity

Working capital is an operating investment:

```{math}
\text{Operating working capital}
=\text{Receivables}
+\text{Inventory}
+\text{Other operating current assets}
-\text{Payables}
-\text{Other operating current liabilities}.
```

Growth can consume cash before it produces accounting profit. A project model
should include initial inventory and receivables, supplier terms, tax timing,
seasonal peaks, and recovery of working capital at the end only when recovery
is economically plausible.

Liquidity is different from profitability. A positive-NPV project can still
create distress if it requires cash before financing is available. Model peak
funding, minimum cash, undrawn committed facilities, debt maturities, covenants,
and collateral—not only annual closing cash.

## Incremental cash-flow rules

Include:

- capital expenditure and installation;
- incremental revenue, operating cost, and cash tax;
- opportunity costs of assets already owned;
- cannibalization or complementarity with existing products;
- changes in operating working capital;
- terminal cleanup, disposal, tax, and recoverable working capital;
- side effects on other projects or contractual obligations.

Exclude sunk costs already incurred and financing cash flows when the discount
rate already reflects financing. Match nominal cash flows with a nominal rate,
real cash flows with a real rate, and currency-specific cash flows with a
currency-consistent rate {cite}`damodaran2012investment`.

## Decision measures

### Net present value

```{math}
\text{NPV}
=-I_0+\sum_{t=1}^{N}\frac{CF_t}{(1+r)^t}.
```

NPV measures estimated value added today and is additive across independent
projects when cash flows and rates are consistent. Financing side effects must
either be reflected in the discount rate or valued separately, not counted in
both places {cite}`myers1974FinancingInvestment`.

### Internal rate of return

IRR is the rate that solves:

```{math}
0=-I_0+\sum_{t=1}^{N}\frac{CF_t}{(1+\text{IRR})^t}.
```

IRR is intuitive but can have multiple or no economically meaningful solutions
when cash-flow signs change more than once. It can also rank mutually exclusive
projects incorrectly because it emphasizes percentage return rather than value
created at scale. IRR itself does not assume that interim cash flows are
reinvested at the IRR; the relevant limitations are non-uniqueness, scale and
timing conflicts with NPV, and the financing or reinvestment policy actually
needed to realize the modeled cash flows {cite}`keane1979IRRReinvestment`.

### Payback and profitability index

Simple payback is the earliest modeled date at which cumulative undiscounted
cash flow becomes non-negative. It is a liquidity and exposure measure, not a
value measure, because it ignores discounting and later cash flows. Interpolated
payback additionally assumes a within-period timing pattern and must not spread
an end-of-period salvage or working-capital recovery uniformly across a year.

```{math}
\text{Profitability index}
=\frac{\text{Present value of later net cash inflows}}{I_0}
=1+\frac{\text{NPV}}{I_0}.
```

This equality assumes a conventional cash-flow pattern: the sole outflow is
$I_0$ at time zero and all later net cash flows are non-negative. If later
cleanup, decommissioning, or other outflows are material, a gross convention is
more transparent:

```{math}
PI_{\mathrm{gross}}
=\frac{PV(CF_t^{+})}{I_0+PV(-CF_t^{-})}.
```

In that case, $PI_{\mathrm{gross}}$ is not generally equal to
$1+\mathrm{NPV}/I_0$. State the numerator and denominator convention and rely
on full NPV rather than a mechanically reported index. The profitability index
can help rank divisible projects under a genuine capital constraint, but it
should not override NPV for indivisible, mutually exclusive projects.

## Worked example: expansion project

A project requires USD 100m of equipment and USD 10m of initial operating
working capital. It generates USD 28m of after-tax operating cash flow in each
of Years 1 through 5. At the end of Year 5, the company recovers the USD 10m of
working capital and receives USD 8m of after-tax salvage proceeds. Use a 10%
annual required return.

The cash-flow vector is:

| Time | Cash flow | Explanation |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| 0 | (110) | Equipment plus working capital |
| 1–4 | 28 each year | After-tax operating cash flow |
| 5 | 46 | Operating cash flow plus working-capital recovery and salvage |

```{math}
\begin{aligned}
\text{NPV}
&=-110+\sum_{t=1}^{5}\frac{28}{1.10^t}
+\frac{18}{1.10^5}\\
&=7.32.
\end{aligned}
```

The project's IRR is approximately 12.39%. Under the annual end-of-year timing
used in the DCF, cumulative undiscounted cash flow first becomes positive at the
end of Year 4, so discrete payback is 4.00 years. If the USD 28m Year 4 operating
cash flow is assumed to accrue uniformly through the year, an explicitly
interpolated estimate is $3+26/28=3.93$ years.

The left panel shows the USD 110m time-zero outlay, USD 28m operating cash flows in Years 1 through 5, and separate USD 18m Year 5 recovery and salvage. The right panel shows NPV declining with the effective annual required return, crossing zero at the 12.39% IRR; NPV at 10% is USD 7.32m.


The profitability index is:

```{math}
1+\frac{7.32}{110}=1.067.
```

The project passes the base-case NPV test, but the conclusion depends on the
operating cash flows, salvage, working-capital recovery, timing, and 10%
required return. A decision memo should show the NPV without salvage and with
slower ramp-up.

## ROIC, growth, and reinvestment

At the operating level:

```{math}
\text{Incremental ROIC}
=\frac{\text{Incremental NOPAT at steady state}}
{\text{Incremental invested capital}}.
```

ROIC is not a substitute for NPV because it can ignore timing and project life.
It is a useful economic diagnostic: growth creates value when incremental ROIC
is expected to exceed the risk-consistent cost of capital. Growth can destroy
value when it requires repeated investment below that threshold
{cite}`damodaran2012investment`.

For a business with stable economics, a useful relationship is:

```{math}
\text{Growth rate}
\approx
\text{Reinvestment rate}\times\text{Return on incremental capital}.
```

The relationship should be estimated from operating drivers, not used to force
a terminal growth rate.

## Real options

Traditional NPV assumes a fixed plan. Management may have the right, but not
the obligation, to:

- **defer** until demand or regulation becomes clearer;
- **stage** spending and stop after an unsuccessful milestone;
- **expand** when adoption exceeds expectations;
- **contract** capacity when demand weakens;
- **switch** inputs, output, geography, or technology;
- **abandon** and recover assets or working capital.

The incremental value of flexibility is measured against the same project
without the state-contingent decision:

```{math}
\text{Real-option value}
=\text{NPV}_{\text{flexible policy}}
-\text{NPV}_{\text{fixed policy}}
\ge 0,
```

when the flexible policy is optimally exercised and retains the fixed action as
an available choice.

Flexibility has more value when uncertainty and irreversibility are high,
management can observe useful information before acting, and the option can be
exercised credibly. Do not add an arbitrary "option premium" to a weak project.
Specify the decision date, observable trigger, exercise cost, operational
ability, and cash-flow consequence {cite}`mcdonaldSiegel1986Waiting`.

## Payout and the capital-allocation decision set

Management can use excess cash to:

1. fund positive-NPV projects and necessary operating resilience;
2. maintain a sustainable capital structure and liquidity reserve;
3. reduce debt or other expensive claims;
4. pay regular or special dividends;
5. repurchase shares;
6. acquire businesses or financial assets.

This list is a decision set, not a universal ranking. An acquisition can be a
positive-NPV investment, debt reduction can dominate a fragile expansion, and a
liquidity reserve can preserve valuable flexibility. Compare alternatives under
the same constraints, timing, risk, and valuation evidence.

Dividends create a direct cash commitment. Repurchases are more flexible but
create value only relative to the price paid, alternative uses, taxes, and
resulting risk. A repurchase below well-supported intrinsic value can benefit
remaining owners; a repurchase above value can transfer wealth to selling
owners. Debt-funded payout can raise short-term per-share metrics while
reducing resilience {cite}`jensen1986AgencyFreeCashFlow,bravEtAl2005Payout`.

## Checks and limitations

- A project discount rate should reflect project risk, not automatically the
  company's current WACC.
- IRR does not impose a reinvestment assumption by itself; it can still have
  multiple solutions or mis-rank mutually exclusive projects because of scale
  and timing differences.
- Working-capital recovery and terminal values are frequently optimistic.
- Forecasted tax benefits may be unusable when taxable income is insufficient.
- Real options require operational rights and decision processes, not only
  uncertainty.
- Stakeholder, legal, environmental, and contractual constraints can make a
  financially attractive project infeasible.

## Practice

A project requires USD 80m of equipment and USD 5m of working capital at time
zero. It generates USD 26m in Years 1 through 3. Year 4 cash flow is USD 40m,
including operating cash flow, working-capital recovery, and after-tax salvage.
Use a 9% required return.

1. Calculate NPV.
2. Estimate IRR and simple payback.
3. Calculate the profitability index.
4. Identify one real option and one governance control that would improve the
   decision.

```{dropdown} Suggested answer
The cash-flow vector is $(-85,26,26,26,40)$. NPV at 9% is approximately USD
9.15m and IRR is approximately 13.49%. After Year 3, USD 78m has been recovered;
because the Year 4 cash flow includes end-of-year working-capital recovery and
salvage, the stated annual model supports a discrete payback of 4.00 years, not
an interpolation across the full USD 40m. The profitability index is
$1+9.15/85=1.108$. A stage gate after early customer evidence could preserve
an option to stop or expand.
Independent review of demand, a post-audit, or approval tied to a funding
threshold would strengthen governance.
```
